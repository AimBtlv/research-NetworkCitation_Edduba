These are the areas for which we have direct evidence for lists and running accounts maintained
on writing boards. Working out from these there are a number of further areas where we can be
fairly confident that this was the case. Thus, complementary to the writing boards covering sheep
there should have been a set dealing with cattle, probably broken down by gender and age. Sets
of writing boards for recording the movements of birds can be expected but are not directly
attested so far.16 There may have been separate series for the various types of birds kept by the
temple (ducks, geese, doves). Iron and bronze were issued for work in the same way as gold, and
we would expect a running ledger to be kept in the same way. In Uruk, Dilbat and Borsippa there
were writing boards covering the overall income (erbu) and outgoings (telitu) of the temples. The
terms erbu and telitu were also used in the administration in Sippar, and there may indeed have
been separate series for incoming and outgoing silver. But with dates and barley it may be that
the transactions were logged according to the class of payment: suitu,i mittu and tithes coming in,
offerings, rations etc. going out. The flow of wool through the administration should also have
been monitored on writing boards, from the initial delivery to the temple after shearing, to
allocations made for clothing allowances, raw wool issued to temple weavers and the finished
product weighed back in. Wool might also be sold; this could be done locally, but it might also
be handed over to a merchant (tamkdru) for a venture further afield (harrdnu); there are no
references from Sippar to writing boards listing goods sent for export, but there is a reference to
a le'u gah arriznii n a text from Babylon.'7 The rkept an up to date record of

Were there any purposes for which writing boards were not suitable? The most obvious area is
contracts. These were traditionally formatted as tablets sealed with cylinder seals and here the
fact that clay could not be written on after drying was its great advantage, preventing alteration
of the contract. Also, Mesopotamian sealing practice was perfectly adapted to use on clay tablets
and clay tablets were particularly well suited to contracts: the sealed tablet could be inspected
without destroying the information. Wooden writing boards could be sealed (with a bulla on
string)33 but the information could not then be viewed without destroying the integrity of the
sealed unit (breaking the seal or cutting the string).

Writing boards were used in the Ebabbara in an administration which also made extensive use
of clay tablets. Clay was considered the natural medium for some records and writing boards for
others. The situation has been well summarised by Symington:

The capacity for updating meant that boards "afforded the freedom of adding, subtraction and
finally erasing information which was no longer needed".35 The advantages of clay tablets were
that they were cheap, could therefore be copied readily, and could be sealed. There was an
interplay of information between these two media, with the consequence that we must look for
at least the following types of practice:
(1) information transferred from clay tablets to wooden boards.
(2) information transferred from wooden boards to clay tablets.
(3) information entered on wooden boards without first being entered on a tablet.
(4) information entered simultaneously on both tablet and writing boards.
(5) deletion of information.
(6) consultation.

(1) Information transferredfromc lay tablets to wooden boards
This procedure is attested for tithes (Text No. 6), sheep (CT 55 482) and dates issued as pappasu

concern (CT 22 11).

Conclusions
Wooden writing boards are attested in the Ebabbara at Sippar from Nabopolassar year 10 to
Xerxes year 1. The principal purposes for which they were used were: (1) running accounts of
offerings, rations and pappasu; agricultural dues (tithes and field rents); seed-grain; sheep and
probably cattle and poultry; issue and receipt of silver and gold (and probably also iron, bronze
and wool); property rentals; and probably also loans of temple stock; and (2) registers of slaves,
workmen, and holders and performers of prebends; and possibly also practical equipment and
cultic utensils. Other purposes for which writing boards may have been used include letters and
recordingg oods entrustedt o a merchant;t he principala reas where we have no reason to think
writing boards were used were short administrative notes within the temple, contracts and records
of court proceedings. Outside of the administrative sphere writing boards were employed for
literature, scholarship (medical, astrological, and omen texts) and rituals. The boards were regularly
consulted to clarify working details and might also be subjected to close examination in the course
of official enquiries, for example in a review of personnel or auditing accounts. To this purpose
they might be kept for many years, though exactly which boards and for how long we do not
know. Given the many areas where we know that writing boards were used and the paucity of
