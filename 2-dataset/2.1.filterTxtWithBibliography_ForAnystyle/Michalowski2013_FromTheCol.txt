FROM THE COLLECTIONS OF AN OLD BABYLONIAN
LITERARY CONNOISSEUR
BY
Piotr MICHALOWSKI
The last two decades have witnessed a tremendous amount of progress in the understanding of Old
Babylonian Sumerian literature and of the scribal curricula of educational establishments in Nippur, Ur,
Uruk, Isin, Larsa, Susa, parts of Sippar and other places that utilized texts chosen in various ways from
the same loose group of compositions.1 Less is known, however, about the circulation of Sumerian
literary compositions in other places, where different choices were often made. Some aspects of the
problem have been discussed in a recent essay by Steve Tinney (2011), who has reconstructed traces of
archives or collections of "non-canonical" Sumerian poetry from a variety of places, including Nippur,
the supposed center of the "standard" cursus, taking us beyond the standard curriculum discussions, and
Anne Lö hnert (2008, 2011) has provided new insights on the geographical distribution and cultural
functions of contemporary liturgical texts. Just recently Ahlena Gadotti and Alexandra Kleinerman
(2011) published a fascinating Old Babylonian tablet, currently housed in the Jonathan and Jeannette
Rosen Ancient Near Eastern Studies Seminar at Cornell University, that appears to be an Akkadian
language request for tablets inscribed with balaģ liturgical laments, including a list of seven that the
unnamed writer had in hand (text A). The authors of the article were able to identify three of the seven
laments, but the remaining four remain unknown. Such poems were not usually part of the curriculum
used in various ways at Nippur and in cities that followed similar pedagogical patterns, and it is unclear if
many of the extant Old Babylonian balaģ tablets come from educational environments or from other
settings. Therefore, it is impossible, at first glance, to contextualize the letter in its proper setting.
Two other recently published tablets must be linked to this letter. The first is a short Sumerian
text from the same Cornell collection, was edited by Gadotti and Kleinerman in a separate article (2012).
The authors obviously first thought that the second tablet contained a list of incipits, but then changed
their minds and suggested that it be read as a continuous five-line Sumerian composition or extract. For
convenience I repeat the transliteration below (text B):

It is possible that these lines were thrown together as an exercise to produce a running text, as
the editors of the tablet suggest. Examples of disparate phrases used as exercises are known from earlier
times, but I am not aware of any Old Babylonian examples. There are a few school exercises that
combine lexical entries and "proverbs," but these are very different from what we are dealing with here
(Krebernik 2004). From Ebla there is an enigmatic composition, at one time taken for a mythological
fragment that is actually collection of personal names and phrases (Edzard 1984: 43-45 [ARET 5
24=25=26]). 2 There may be three other Early Dynastic exercises of this type from Lagash/Al-Hiba, one
listing phrases containing the name of the god Enlil and two others, and possibly two more with
miscellaneous expressions.3
More probably, their first hunch was correct and this is a brief list of literary tablets used for
some purpose by someone named Ili-ippalsam. This would not be the first time that a list of literary
incipits has been taken for something else. Over three decades ago I was surprised that I could identify a
Sumerian literary "catalog" in the University Museum in Philadelphia, since Sam Kramer and many other
experts had combed through the collection so many times. The only explanation I could offer was that the
tablet in question was written in the "documentary hand" and was thus taken for an administrative text,
and therefore of no interest to literary specialists (Michalowski 1980). More recently, Jeremiah Peterson
(2010) demonstrated that TCL 15 17, edited by Äke Sjöberg (1960: 65-69) and Mark Hall (1985: 560-
61) as a Nanna hymn, has to be reinterpreted as a list of Sumerian incipits.
The first line is the opening of the letter or petition (letter-prayer) to Enki edited by W. W. Hallo
(1968: 82-88) on the basis of three unpublished Yale tablets of unknown origin, possibly from Larsa or
vicinity. A fourth fragmentary duplicate was discovered at Meturan/Tell Haddad (Cavigneaux 1998: 253,
Brisch 2007: 82). The second line must be the incipit of a short "folktale about grain milling" that is
known form a single exemplar, also of unknown origin, which must be restored accordingly (Civil 2006:
138).
Line 3 was read as u-sal i-ru-u in the original edition and translated as "hides in the meadow
(instead)," taking ru as a variant of ri, "to lay down." This is certainly plausible; another way of
interpreting this would be to read the line in Akkadian as aburrís ni-ru-ú or the like. The main objection
to this lies in the fact that almost all forms of the verb rê'û , "to tend cattle, graze, pasture," retain the /e/
vowel.
Line 4 is probably an abbreviation of 1. 12 of the taunt composition "Good Seed of A Dog" (a
duio-ga ur-ra), known only from two Nippur sources: ní-zuh é buru3-buru3 êlsig gub-bu za-ra suh-ù, "He is
a thief, constantly breaking into houses, removing the door, tearing out the door-pivot" (Sjöberg 1972:
107). Line 5 is probably another extract from an Akkadian composition, while line 6 must be from an
"eduba'a essay" that is presently unknown to us. For the latter, compare, for example, um-mi-a á-ág-gá é-
dub-ba-a èn tar-ri-da-ni, "Once the master consulted the regulations of the teaching establishment ..."
Schooldays 28 (Kramer 1949: 204-5; Edzard 2004: 533-4), or á-ág-gá é-dub-ba-a um-mi-a, in Eduba'a
R iv 25 (unp., courtesy A. Gadotti and A. Kleinerman). Note that the writer was getting sloppy at the end,
or perhaps was worried about the length of line 6; thus we have á-<ág>-gá at the beginning and at the end
ak for ka.

ippalsam. The name is not unknown in Old Babylonian documents.4 One of the more interesting

What does this tablet trio tell us about literary life in Old Babylonian times? When confronted
by list of incipits or an imgida with a model contract, one immediately thinks of an educational setting.
But text A is an Akkadian language request for tablets containing Sumerian liturgical poems and the
author had in mind a specific set of twenty-one such texts. The geographical setting is unknown, and it is
possible that these were necessary for instructional purposes, but it is equally probable that the writer had
ritual performance or his own library collection in mind. This, in turn, may suggest that the incipit list, as
well as the model contract, which were both written by the person who made this request and not by a
student, were likewise part of an eclectic tablet collection that may not necessarily have been intended for
instructional purposes. Although it would be good to think that this person was none other than the Ili-
ippalsam/Ili-ippalsani whose name figures in the subscripts of texts B and C. But however one sees it, it
is fascinating to observe that this collector was interested in Emesal register balaģ liturgical texts as well
as somewhat obscure main dialect Sumerian compositions such as the letter of petition to Enki and the
millstone story and in hitherto unidentified Akkadian texts. Was he also skilled in music, as the model
contract would suggest, or was his name added at the end only as a piece of fancy, with an equally
fanciful title of um-mi-a?
Ili-ippalsam was forgotten for millennia and now that we have retrieved his memory, we have
still only lifted a tiny corner of the veil suspended over his life by the mists of time. Perhaps he had a
substantial collection of mainstream Old Babylonian literary tablets and the lists revealed to us only
concern marginalia that he needed to complete his holdings, but it is also possible that he eschewed the
well-treaded school texts that he had committed to memory during his youth and was only interested in
contemporary literary exotica. Such speculations immediately bring to mind the Old Babylonian
epigraphic discoveries from Meturan, that is Tell Haddad in the Hamrin Basin, as described by Antoine
Cavigneaux (1998). One of the houses in the city contained two different sets of literary tablets in
different rooms. One room revealed standard literary materials such as "Inana and Ebih," four of the five
standard Sumerian Gilgamesh stories, "The Farmer's Instructions," divine hymns, literary letters, etc.,
while the other divulged magical and hemerological texts written in a somewhat different hand. With all
due caution, Cavigneaux (1998: 257) suggested that this house contained something akin to a "library" of
"'a Fachman,' of a man sensitive in the realms of religion and literature; a man we would now like even
more to identify and know better, an intellectual with universal and perhaps even historical interests." Ili-
ippalsam may have been a kindred soul, and there were doubtless more such people, who evidently
stayed in touch, exchanging tablets from their collections. We do not know where his tablets come from
except that they are not from Nippur, Ur, or Uruk, but that still does not allow us to label them as
provincial, rather, they confirm Steve Tinney' s work on the multiple settings of Old Babylonian
literature, which radiated far beyond the limits of childhood education. One could speculate that much the
same could be said of the person associated with the list of literary incipits CBS 8086 from the
Philadelphia museum that included main curriculum items such as the "Kesh Temple Hymn" as well
liturgical texts and compositions that are unknown to us at present (Michalowski 1980). Like Text B
from the Ili-ippalsam group, this list includes lines that are not incipits of compositions but of extract
tablets and therefore may have some connection with education, but the content suggests that it was not
part of the regular Nippur cursus and therefore may have belonged to some collector, perhaps a colleague
of the persons who accumulated the "hymnic archive" from Nippur, as reconstructed by Steve Tinney
(2011: 585) or the similar collection of unknown origin, now in the British Museum, likewise identified
by Tinney (2011: 586). Other texts among those that have been grouped together as "literary catalogs"
enumerate heterogeneous lists of incipits that may have originated in non-pedagogical contexts.10 One
such Old Babylonian "catalog" is written on an unprovenanced tablet in the holdings of Andrews

ABSTRACT
A reexamination of three recently published texts associated with a scholar / scribe / teacher / collector by the name
of Ili-ippalsam or Ili-ippalsani who lived somewhere in Babylonia during the middle of the Old Babylonian period
provides an opportunity for a discussion of matters pertaining to literary tablet circulation at the time.
RÉSUMÉ
Un réexamen de trois textes publiés récemment associés à un savant / scribe / enseignant / collectionneur du nom de
Ili-ippalsam ou Ili-ippalsani qui vivait quelque part en Babylonie pendant le milieu de la période paléo-babylonienne
fournit l'occasion d'une discussion sur les questions relatives à la circulation de la tablette littéraire à l'époque.
