LIPIT-ESTAR'S PRAISE IN THE EDUBBA
H. L. J. Vanstiphout
Rifksuniversiteit Groningen
1. Introduction
The hymn edited below,1 also known as Li B (Lipit-ettar Hymn B) or
"Lipit-7star, King of Justice, Wisdom and Learning,"2 is not mentioned in
any catalogue so far, although it seems to have been very popular as an
exercise text in schools. Its relative brevity (60-odd lines) and clarity may
partly account for this.
The hymn was first treated by H. de Genouillac in RA 25 (1928) 149-54;
he only used the Louvre text (our text A) and one fragment of our text B
(CBS 13967=PBS 5 67=PBS 13 49). R. Jestin reedited the text in RA 40
(1945) 47-54, and gave a few philological notes. A. Falkenstein did a
splendid translation in SAHG (1953) pp. 123-26, and W. Rimer edited the
text in Konigshymnen (1965) pp. 23-29, with translation, apparatus, and
lexical notes, using our text A, the published fragment of text B, texts C, F,
G, and school "buns" S1, S2, and S8. Many more fragments have since been
identified, mainly in the tablet collections of the University Museum in
Philadelphia. During a visit to the University Museum in April 1977,3 1 was
able to join six fragments to CBS 13967, thus obtaining an almost complete
tablet originally containing the whole composition (see pls. 1 and 2). Some
other texts seem to be fragments of other complete recensions, a few are
school exercises of different formats, and there are now six more school
"buns." All this new material, but especially the reconstructed tablet B as a
virtually complete Nippur text, justifies a new edition.
1. Prof. A. W. Sjdberg not only entrusted me with the edition of this hymn and of Li A
(forthcoming), but also gave generously of his time and learning in discussing several details.
To him, as to everybody in the University Museum Tablet Room, go my heartfelt thanks. The
photographs were taken by G. Haayer, and the excellent hand copy on pl. 4 is by G. Farber-
Fhlgge. The following abbreviations for Sumerian literary compositions have been used in the
commentary and/or footnotes: Li A: Lipit-e~tar Hymn A: see provisionally R6mer,
K6nigshymnen pp. 29-38; ELA: Enmerkar and the Lord of Aratta: see n. 9 below; LU:
Lamentation over the Destruction of Ur: see provisionally Wilcke, Kollationen p. 57; LN:
Lamentation over the Destruction of Nippur: see provisionally Wilcke, Kollationen p. 54;
LSU: Lamentation over Sumer and Ur: see provisionally Wilcke, Kollationen p. 59; Sulgi B:
Sulgi Hymn B: see provisionally Castellino, Two Sulgi Hymns (Rome, 1972).
2. See S. N. Kramer, ISET 1, Introduction p. 37.
3. The Faculty of Letters of Groningen University provided me with a generous grant for
this visit. Some other results will be published in the near future, including a revised edition of

2. The Manuscripts
Text A: AO 8863 (TCL 16 87) is a hexagonal prism containing the
complete text. It was found in Larsa, and bears the date iti Se-gur10-ku5 /
mu sa-am-su-i-lu-na lugal-e / ugnim ya-mu-ut-ba-lum, i.e., Month
Addaru, 10th year of Samsu-iluna.4 The long lines are broken up into
shorter bits. Dub-sag-X precedes the first line, probably indicating that
the tablet used as a model for this prism started its obverse here (see SL
138,46a and AHw 2 926 s.v. qudmu="front side").
Text B: tablet from Nippur containing the whole composition,
reconstructed from CBS 13967 (PBS 5 67=PBS 13 49) + N 1509 +
N 1537 + N 1553 + N 1739 + N 4176 + N 6282. This six-column
tablet also breaks up the lines, but not exactly in the same way as A.
Text H: UM 29-16-145, from Nippur, is a fragment of a six-column tablet
originally containing the complete text; subdivision of lines nearly as in
B.
Text E: CBS 13380 could be another fragment of a "complete" edition,
but the state of preservation of the reverse precludes any identification;
disposition of lines and columns nearly as B and H.
Text F: Ni 1601 (ISET 1 48=106) is a school tablet which originally
contained the whole text on its obverse(?) and devoted the rest of the
tablet to a lexical text; the hymn is divided over three columns and the
lines are broken up.
Text C: CBS 14134 (STVC 78) is an oblong school extract, originally
containing lines 1 through 19; most lines are left intact.
Text G: Ni 3042 (SLTN 69) is a school extract of the same type as C: it is
square or oblong in form, and has intact lines; the reverse is not
inscribed.
Text I: CBS 6668 is also a squarish school extract; the lines are broken up,
the script is rather crude, and the reverse is a lexical exercise.
Text J: CBS 6943 is of the same type as I: a square school extract, with
crude script and broken lines. I cannot place the reverse(?).
Texts S1-S,: "bun"-shaped school exercise tablets, on which the teacher
wrote one line on top which the student had to copy underneath. All
texts, except the duplicates S7=Ss from Ur, come from Nippur.
4. See TCL 16, Introduction pp. 11-12.

LIPIT-ESTAR'S PRAISE 37
5. Translation5
1. Lipiteastar, proud king, enthroned prince,
2. Most fit offshoot of kingship,
3. Who walks like Utu, brilliant light of the Land,
4. Lofty in nobility, riding the great ordinances,
5. Who settles the people in the four quarters,
6. Favoured by Enlil, beloved by Inanna,
7. True youth with shining eyes, worthy of the throne
8. (Whose) head is adorned with the tiara, the true crown,
9. (Who is) perfectly equipped with the sceptre over the Blackheads,
10. Prince Lipit-estar, son of Enlil, wise shepherd,
11. Who leads the people into sweet shadow, relaxing moonlight?,
12. Lord, great bison, beloved by An,
13. Your trust is (put in) the mother of Ninlil.
14. Lipit-estar, you are granted princely power;
15. (Your) name, a sound as sweet as honey, is perfect for speaking;
16. Longed-for husband of Inanna,
17. Whom Enki gave broad wisdom as a gift.
18. Nisaba, the woman radiant with joy,
19. The true woman scribe, the lady of all knowledge,
20. Guided your fingers on the clay,
21. Embellished the writing on the tablets,
22. Made the hand resplendent with a golden stylus.
23. The measuring rod, the gleaming surveyor's line,
24. The cubit ruler which gives wisdom,
24a. Nisaba lavishly bestowed upon you.
25. Lipit-estar, you are Enlil's son;
26. Truth and justice you make manifest;
27. Lord, your goodness covers even the horizon.
28. King Lipit-e'tar, counsellor of great judgment,
29. (Whose) word never falters, wise one (whose) decision provides
justice for the people;
30. Great mind, knowing all things deeply,
31. In order to lay down the law for all foreign countries,
32. You perceive falsehood and truth even from the words hidden in the
heart.
33. Lipit-estar, you rage against the enemies,
34. From evil and oppression you know how to save people,
35. From sin and destruction you know how to free them.
5. Italics indicate tentative translations (or guesses).

8. Some observations
a. Structure
The hymn is most naturally divided into six sections of uneven length.
Section A (lines 1-17) is a loosely arranged string of stock phrases,
mentioning Lipit-e~tar's royalty and the favors several gods have shown
him. There seems to be some method in it: starting with his legitimacy, the
theme of Justice seems to be anticipated in lines 3-5, followed by the royal
insignia (lines 7-9), and the ensuing peace in Sumer (lines 10-11). The
section closes with his relation to An, to Ninlil's mother (? see commentary
to line 13), anticipating section B, to Inanna, and to Enki-whose gift
(wisdom) also anticipates sections B and C.
Section B (lines 18-24a) describes in particular the gifts he received from
Nisaba, i.e., writing and measuring or generally learning and wisdom.
Section C (lines 25-39) describes how he established truth and justice
throughout Sumer and even the foreign countries. It is remarkable that
truth and justice here are viewed rather as manifestations of wisdom than
as administrative measures, and that this wisdom also shows a moral
preoccupation (lines 30-32 and 34-37), linking this section to the previous
one.
Section D (lines 40a50a) embroiders upon the system of stock epithets
from the Royal Inscriptions, describing his relation to Isin, Nippur, KeS, Ur,
Eridu, Uruk, and Isin again.20 Here again scribal art (or learning) has a
special position: he is Nippur's scribe, and if we may combine lines 41-42a,
we could perhaps say that he is the provider of the Ekur because he is its
main scribe (bookkeeper?).
Section E (lines 51-56) alludes to his appointment by his father lsme-
dagan-which could point to the redaction of this hymn at an early stage in
Lipit-etar's reign.2'
Section F (lines 57-63) stresses the main point of the hymn, which is the
glory of Lipit-e~tar, ostensibly because of his promulgation of just laws

It should be noted that the sections are not kept apart very strictly:
section B naturally follows from section A, in that Nisaba is the last one in
the series of deities enumerated in A; also the theme of justice (section C) is
regarded as an instance of his wisdom and learning (and Edubba
education?). Lastly, the concluding section harks back to sections B and C,
combining the Edubba motif with justice and glory.
One often gets the impression, especially in sections A and C, that the
individual lines (or two-line units) are put together without much feeling
for structure, as if the composer was more interested in collecting, rather
arbitrarily, a more or less representative series of "typical" lines. The
subdivision of these lines seems to be based upon the basic structure of the
Sumerian sentence in that it separates the noun phrases (including epithets)
from predicates (either full verbs or copula-constructions).
b. Function
Ostensibly, the function is simply the glorification of Lipit-ettar,
particularly as a king of learning and justice. But the insistence on the
Edubba education is very great. Also the number of beginners' school
copies is striking (see list of MSS), as is the relative absence of "difficult"
constructions (the number of fully inflected verbs is only 23, i.e., about one
in three lines). Indeed, the text may be said to present a fair sample of the
several possibilities of verbal inflection in Sumerian, and these are about
evenly distributed (copula-constructions, "participia" or nominalized
verbs, inflected verbs), as it also seems to offer a sample of typicalhymnal
phrases. Furthermore, the subdivision of the lines could perhaps be
interpreted partly as providing a tentative means of parsing the sentences.
These observations, combined with the theme of the important sections
B, C, and F, lead me to believe that the hymn was composed in and
primarily for the Edubba, to be used there as a beginner's text. The teacher
who composed it would certainly be happy to know that now, after more
than 3700 years, modern Ummia's still find it very suitable for teaching
first-year Sumerian. The wish expressed in lines 59-61 has come true.
