The Jubilarian has faced difficult decisions about text and manuscript in his pioneering treatment of the Old Akkadian inscriptions. There, Old Babylonian and later copies of Sargonic inscriptions were of crucial importance to the corpus.' Copies, new compositions in antique styles, outright forgeries of earlier texts and objects, and retrospective accounts

of ancient times testify to enduring interest in early periods among Mesopotamian scholars, especially after the mid-first millennium B.C2 Two complementary aspects of this agenda included genuine older texts carefully preserved in later copies, such as Old Babylonian literature,’ and late texts composed and copied in older styles. ,

Certain problematic texts may belong to one or the other of these categories. The objects thus resemble the cones of Sin-kashid of Uruk and other smaller Old Babylonian cones. The thick ends (so far as I can determine) were all pierced with a tapering hole on the long axis, about 1.8 cm in diameter at the surface and about-6.5 cm deep. The hole of (f) is 2 cm in diameter and goes nearly through the object. Partial piercing of this type is found in Late Babylonian commemorative cylinders such as those of Nebuchadnezzar IJ. It can also occur with Old Babylonian cones, such as ~ YBC 2296 (Samsuiluna C). Since this information is generally omitted in _ publications of texts, I have not tried to determine how widespread the -practice was. In some objects of this type, a trace in the clay suggests that a similar hole might have been filled in later. These holes were presumably used to hold the object on a pointed staff while writing on it. The barrels are ruled freehand and inscribed along the long axis, beginning at the larger, pierced end, the standard practice with OldBabylonian cylinders and "nails", which, so far as I am aware, always begin the writing at the wider end of the object. Exceptions to this are (c) and (f), which begin the writing at the smaller end. In these, contrary to Old Babylonian practice, the signs are placed between the lines rather than hung from the line above. The characters are generally good Old Babylonian writing, so good as to deceive early masters. like Schroeder and Deimel. Among archaizing features are pseudoarchaic spellings of the demonstrative article: a-ni-a-am and a-ni-i-tam, the Old Babylonian spelling of i-im-ma-ru-ma in two exemplars, and hypercorrect spelling of si-a-tim alongside sa-a-ti in five exemplars (though with variation between um sidtim and umi sidtim). Writings such as GA for qd and RUM (ri) for ru are well known from the Late Babylonian royal inscriptions. Use of UHI for uf in four exemplars may have been intended to look archaic or old fashioned as well. The absence of mimation, especially in lines 15 (saplati) and 16 (zakuti) for all exemplars, and in some exemplars for lines 3 and 8/1 and 8/2 (annita versus annitam), undermines the Old Babylonian effect, and was perhaps owed to the frequent use of these words in SB literature, so. the scribes might have unconsciously written them in more familiar form. The scribes used awilum as the Old Babylonian form for amélu, but /iltd rather than *lisi. All scribes wrote a less common word, usassak, without doubling the /s/, in the manner of an old inscription, but all wrote igabbii with a doubled consonant, as the word was a common one in everyday writing. Scribe (a) opted for a syllabic spelling of limurma, while the others used a spelling less likely for Old Babylonian. Scribes (d) and (e) represented the verb in line 8 in good Old Babylonian spelling, but not the others. Perhaps (d) and (e) collaborated with each other, making for a symmetry of the two best and the two worst students in the class doing their schoolwork together. One must leave to the imagination the question as to whether these young scholars were working in the classroom, stealing a glance at the cylinder next to them from time to time, or were preparing their inscriptions at home, in which case one might see here evidence of friends, family or _ neighbors sharing their assignments.

Epigraphy and orthography are by no means the only features of interest in this text; its high literary quality sets it well apart from the small corpus to which Bottéro, Delitzsch, and Thureau-Dangin have assigned it. One may at least speculate that some grammatical phenomena may have been part of the teaching process, such as the precative with a future sense (line 6, see von Soden, GAG §158c), which might have called for comment, as well as what might have been meant as an affirmative subjunctive(?) in line 17, unless, as seems more likely, efemmu was construed as a plural, perhaps implying all the ghosts of his family. Bottéro and CAD, for example, understand the noun as singular .. and leaye the plural verb unexplained, while AHw takes the noun as plural.’° For a "set piece" this has remarkable appeal. As to whether a genuine Old Babylonian text lies behind its language, or it is a late composition, I would choose the second possibility, based on subjective impression and on expressions such as mé zakiiti which are not, to my knowledge, attested for Old Babylonian, but the evidence seems too slight for a firm decision.

_ I suggest that all exemplars were in fact written by different scribes, _evidently for an "Advanced Akkadian" class. The variants are explainable because the master dictated the text and his students wrote it down from -What they heard. Most heard the same thing, but some did not include final pronouns, for example, as in lines 7 and 12 (only one, e, left out the final pronoun in both lines, not collated). The students then prepared - cylinders, in somewhat different sizes, in the old style as they had learned it,.and wrote out the text on them in Old Babylonian commemorative signs. A challenging part of the assignment was planning the disposition of the words in the cases on the cylinder, and here the scribes made different decisions, as the variants show, (a) for example having lines 9 and 17 as separate lines, but not 14, although scribes b, c, and d made the verb of 14 a separate line. Scribes (c) and (f) made the spectacular mistake of having the writing go in the wrong direction on the object, and scribe (c) left out a line by mistake. No obvious cases of two cylinders being written by the same scribe present themselves, though one is at a great disadvantage not being able to compare them directly. Some texts, however, seem closely related to each other but not to the others, as if a - student was looking at what his comrade was doing or two students were working together: (d) and (e) share the good archaic spelling, the same forms in lines 3, 7, and 11, but different spellings and use of the final pronoun in lines 4 and 12. Texts (a) and (d) share many features but have different handwritings. The clearest instance of common work is (c) and’ (f), which have a major mistake in common, while (c) adds another mistake that (f) avoided. The two are different in line 12, however, and - _ arrange the words differently. The hole of text (f) is more deeply drilled.
