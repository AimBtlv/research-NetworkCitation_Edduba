PURITY AND ACCESS: A CATALOG OF
LEXICAL TEXTS DEDICATED TO NABÛ
Niek Veldhuis (University of California, Berkeley)
This article discusses a Late Babylonian school text from Babylon (CTMMA 2 65 = Gesche 2005) that lists on
the obverse the opening lines of lexical texts, ranging from Ura to Nabnitu, with a dedicatory prayer to Nabû, the
god of writing, on the reverse. The lexical catalog offers a complete overview of the entire lexical corpus of the
time. The prayer invites a discussion of the educational, religious, and social context of the tablet and of scribal
education in the Late Babylonian period.1
Late Babylonian school exercises with a dedicatory prayer to Nabû are well known; they usually appear on large
multicolumn exercise texts (Type 1), which represent the most elementary level of scribal education. Contents
and layout of Type 1 school exercises have been discussed and described in full detail by Gesche (2001: 44–49 and
61–171). The obverse of a Type 1 tablet is typically inscribed with sequences of DIŠ.AŠ.U (that is, the main com-
ponents of cuneiform signs); sequences of A; and passages from the most elementary scribal exercises: Sa and Sb
(signs only); Weidner God List; and Ura 1 and 2. The reverse of a Type 1 exercise may include various nonstandar-
dized exercises (names, exercises in Akkadian orthography, etc.) and a dedicatory prayer, like the one in our text.
More advanced Late Babylonian exercises are found on single-column practice tablets (Type 2) that are divided
into small sections (4 to 7 lines) by horizontal rulings. Type 2 exercises typically contain short extracts from lite-
rary and religious texts, followed by a sequence of extracts from lexical texts. Type 2 exercise tablets have also been
studied by Gesche (2001: 49–52 and 172–98), distinguishing between several subtypes.
In her edition, Gesche (2005: 257) demonstrated that CTMMA 2 65 belongs to a group of four Late Babylonian
Type 1 exercises that share a number of unusual features. First, they are smaller than regular Type 1 tablets, with
only one column of text on the obverse; second, they turn like the page of a book; and third, the exercises on the
obverse do not follow the established patterns.2 Each of these has a dedication to Nabû on the reverse; the two best-
preserved examples show that Nabû ša nikkassi, a Babylon manifestation of Nabû, was the recipient.

The Neo- or Late Babylonian lexical corpus thus reconstructed differs in several respects from its Neo-Assyrian
counterpart. A number of lexical compositions that are well known from kuyunjik and other Neo-Assyrian tablet
collections (Assur, Nimrud, Sultan Tepe) are missing from the list and those are, indeed, never found in Neo- or
Late Babylonian exercises and only very rarely as (Babylonian) library exemplars. Those compositions include
the Emesal vocabulary (rare in Babylonia),11 Antaĝal (very rare),12 acrographic lists (rare); Idu (absent), Lanu
(absent); Igidu (absent); and the Practical vocabulary of Assur (absent). Murgud, the commentary to Ura, is the
only exception to this rule. This composition does not appear in the Babylon exercise, nor in the Uruk catalog but
is, in fact, well known in the tablet collections from Late Babylonian Uruk and Babylon.

Even though this pupil was able to produce elegant writing, and he was competent enough to write or copy a com-
plex prayer in Akkadian, he seems to have been out of his comfort zone when it came to the rare words and less
frequent signs that one encounters in Malku or Erimhuš. The long list of incipits is not a testament to the know-
ledge that this scribe had acquired, but rather a vision of the erudition he hoped to reach.
how did this vision work out—what did our student learn in his subsequent lessons and how did he learn it?
Rather than copying and learning one lexical tablet at a time, students wrote out a number of brief passages of
consecutive chapters of a lexical series. The intermediate level of Neo-Babylonian education is represented by
single column Type 2 exercise tablets. Typically, such tablets begin with a few lines from a (bilingual) incantation
series, continue with a brief section from a literary text (a prayer or Enuma Eliš) and finish with a series of brief
sections, usually four to seven lines each, from a sequence of lexical texts (Gesche 2001: 172–98). By far the most
frequently extracted lexical text is the thematic list Ura, but we also find the list of professions Lu ; the list of (Akka-
2

An = Anum; and the word list Nabnitu21—in other words, all the lists in the catalog above.

The chapters 3–24 of the thematic list Ura were learned by writing long sequences of Type 2 exercise tablets, a
few lines at a time. By analyzing the contents of hundreds of such extract tablets, Gesche 2001 was able to show that
students gradually added higher number chapters of the Ura series. An example is BM 54203 = P349571 (Gesche
2001: 387) which, after extracts from a bilingual incantation and a prayer to Marduk includes extracts from Ura
10–17 as follows:
Ura 10 (earthenware) 421–427
Ura 13 (domestic animals) 76–81
Ura 14 (wild animals) 48–53
Ura 15 (meat cuts) 13–18
Ura 16 (stones) 9–13
Ura 17 (plants) 1–5

3. Social and Religious Context
As far as I know, the scribe who wrote the dedicatory prayer to Nabû in CTMMA 2 65, PN son of Bēl-balassu-
iqbi, is not known from elsewhere, but other such dedication texts are more forthcoming. MS 5007, a very ele-
mentary exercise published by George (2010: 274–79), concludes with the prayer of Šamaš-rīhtu-usur, son of
Šamaš-iddina the Baker of Šamaš and Aya. As George points out, the father is known from the Ebabbar archives
of Sippar as holder of a baker’s prebend and is discussed by Bongenaar (1997: 197). The pupil who wrote MS 5007
thus belonged to one of the prebendary families of Sippar—and that is not surprising.
During the Neo- and Late Babylonian period a number of elite families dominated the priesthood at the impor-
tant temples in each of the Babylonian cities (Waerzeggers 2011). “Priests,” in Waerzeggers’ terminology, included
caterers (bakers, butchers, fishermen, etc.), ritual specialists (singers, acrobats, exorcists), artisans (reed workers,
potters), and what one may call staff (measurers, scribes, gatekeepers). All of these functions contributed to the
daily care of the gods,32 which was considered the essential all-important function of the temple organization that
was performed, at least in theory, under direct responsibility of the king, the ultimate provider for the gods. These
tasks were organized through a prebendary system by which a priest received remuneration in exchange for a cer-
tain number of days of service. Prebendary offices were reserved for a few elite families in each city; it was in these
circles of prebendaries that the cuneiform tradition was maintained.
A prebendary baker, like Šamaš-iddina, was bound by rules of purity and access. he was expected to punctually
prepare the bread for the god’s meal and hand that over in a presentation ceremony in the temple courtyard to a
priest of higher rank whose task it was to bring the food to the god. The baker’s status as a mid-level priest was spa-
tially delimited by his access to the courtyard—but not beyond. The purity of the divine meal not only depended
on the purity of the ingredients and the finished food, but also on the correct lineage and moral and physical
attributes of the baker himself.33
The dedications of school texts to Nabû touch upon the two defining aspects of priesthood: purity and access.
The tablet dedicated to Nabû was made from pure clay that was not taken from the regular supply, but from a
location outside of the city. The emphasis on pure clay connects directly to the social and professional context in
which these pupils grew up. Secondly, the pupil presented his work not directly to the god, but rather left it at the
doorman in a gunnu container, not unlike the prebendary baker who presented the finished food in a ceremony
at the temple courtyard.
The dedication prayer thus illustrates how writing and education were part of the conceptual world that regu-
lated the functioning of the temples and the prebendary families who used their prebends, as well as their literacy,
to acquire and maintain their elite position in the city.
