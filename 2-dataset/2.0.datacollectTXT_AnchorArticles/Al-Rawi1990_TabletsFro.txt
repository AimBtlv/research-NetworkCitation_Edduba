II. TABLET II OF THE BABYLONIAN CREATION EPIC
By f. ?. h. AL-RAwi* and a. r. George
As work continues on the Neo-Babylonian library recently discovered within the precincts
of the temple of Shamash at Sippar, it is becoming clearer that much of its contents is
already well known to us.1 Only a very small number of completely new texts have been
identified so far, and our reaction to the material retrieved is at present much as that of a
previous generation of scholars to the Sultantepe tablets.2 We are confirmed in our belief
that the major products of the Babylonian literary tradition are, in varying states of
preservation, already to hand. A corollary of this belief is that future discoveries of Neo-
Babylonian libraries should be expected to yield more literature not so much by furnishing
new literary texts as by filling the lacunae in texts of the established corpus. It goes without
saying that since many known texts are still in a very poor state of preservation this is an
exciting prospect.
The importance of the Sippar library, then, will principally lie in its capacity to restore
known but damaged texts to a more satisfactory state of completion. This capacity is already
well demonstrated by the inauguration of this series of texts from the Sippar library with a
surprising new source for the composition previously known as the Weidner Chronicle.3 The
present article continues this pattern, though with less unforeseen results, by publishing a
manuscript of Tablet II of the Babylonian Creation Epic. The tablet came from niche 5 C of
the library?that is the third niche from the ground in the fifth row from the left.4
En?ma elis II has remained until now the least well preserved of the Seven Tablets of
Creation, the middle part being very badly damaged.5 Four sections of disconnected text
were separated, as it seemed, by three gaps of uncertain length. The line-count of the Tablet
was itself unknown.

Tablet.6 The substance of this episode of the Creation Epic is the initial efforts of the gods,
under their king Anshar, to defend themselves against the threat of imminent destruction at
the hands of their ancestral mother Ti'amat, who, incited by the assembly of rebel gods, has
taken the war-path against her offspring. The newly restored middle portion of the Tablet
(11. 49-118) describes in detail what was previously known only in outline, that first Ea and
then Anu go against Ti'amat, but both return in terror. This passage is most notable for the
interview between Anshar, angry at the news of Ti'amat's approach, and the cunning Ea,
who is blamed for being the cause of her hostile mood. Anshar accuses Ea bitterly, arguing
that his primeval heroics in slaying Ti'amat's consort, Aps?, have brought on the present
crisis (11. 49-56). Ea replies, soothing the mood of Anshar, and reminding him that were it
not for the death of Aps? the present order would not have been established (57-70). Anshar
is thus prompted to recall Ea's prowess in battle, and in an abrupt turn of the tables
despatches him to destroy the new enemy (71-78). As Ea's skilful words at first get him out
of trouble and then in again, with the use against him of his own argument, one senses a
subtle humour in the composer's mind: Ea will get his come-uppance. The following
passage, describing in turn Ea and Anu returning in failure from confrontation with the
enemy, is marked by lengthy repetition, the greatest virtue of which lies in allowing the
complete restoration of most of the many damaged lines (79-118).
With a new edition of the whole Creation Epic keenly expected from W. G. Lambert, this
article is limited to the presentation of the new source in cuneiform copy and edition, with
observations on the already known text only where there is significant correction.7
