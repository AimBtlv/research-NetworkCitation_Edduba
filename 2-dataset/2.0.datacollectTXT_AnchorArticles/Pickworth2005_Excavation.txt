EXCAVATIONS AT NINEVEH: THE HALZI GATE'
By DIANA PICKWORTH
Abstract
The University of California Expedition to Nineveh excavated at the Halzi Gate in 1989 and 1990. The gate
proved to be one of Nineveh's larger defensive gates and a scene of chaos and destruction. Tower 4 to the
south of the gate was of irregular masonry, having been hastily rebuilt. The juxtaposition of a rough tower
directly beside the fine ashlar masonry of the rest of the curtain wall of the gate appears to reflect a political
instability that prevailed during the declining years of Assyrian rule. The late reconstruction of Tower 4
contrasts with the earlier attention to detail seen in building work of Sennacherib's reign (704-681 BC), when
smoothly dressed orthostats lined the lower walls of the gate's large inner court and sophisticated waterproof
membranes of bitumen-soaked reeds protected the mud-brick superstructures. Buried beneath the pavement
in the corners of the court were apotropaic foundation figurines. Within the only partially excavated outer
entrance passage at least twelve individuals died, perhaps while defending the city or trying to escape from
it, as a stallion and a rider lay at the eastern limit of the gate passage. Within the reduced width of the gate
passage were eight adult males, four adolescents and four children including one infant. Weaponry was found
throughout the area and the scattered bodies lay where they had fallen. The associated small finds, which
had not been looted, included silver jewelry, bronze personal items, stamp seals and a composite necklace.
Introduction
The Halzi Gate was an inauspicious location to be in August of 612 BC. Evidence of the horrors
of war and the demise of the Assyrians at Nineveh was graphically provided at the Halzi Gate
(Fig. 1). This brief interim report is ordered following the sequence of excavation and the work
is presented chronologically.2 Analysis of the excavated skeletal material and the preparation of
various reports is ongoing.3
The Halzi Gate (Reade 2000: 401) is the most southerly gate in the five-kilometre long eastern
wall and it proved to be one of the largest of the city's fifteen external gates (Fig. 2). The walls,
fifteen metres thick, are estimated to have risen to at least twenty metres in height on the eastern
side, where they are situated inside a series of defensive ditches (Stronach 1975: 311). The name
Halzi refers to a province located southeast of Nineveh on the way to Kalhu (modern Nimrud),
on the east bank of the Tigris. It was possibly also the name of a town, and may in some instances
simply to refer to a district.4 Halzi is not to be confused with Barhalzi, an area immediately
outside the walls on the western edge of Nineveh (Parpola 1970: 144). The gate and road were
probably named to reflect the destination. Dr Tariq Mahdloum conducted the first probe at the
Halzi Gate in 1965. It consisted of a narrow, shallow exploratory trench trending east-west that
did not reach the pavement within the inner gate passage. Accordingly, the excavator was unaware
of the slain individuals in the passage.5
The boundaries of the walled city of Nineveh, as they stood in the Neo-Assyrian period, were
redefined by the Department of Antiquities during a five-year period beginning in 1965 (Madhloum
1968: 45). Within that time the work of Dr Mahdloum was a vital contribution toward the
protection of the vast seven-hundred-hectare site as a unit, protecting the tell from further

Outer entrance passage
Following survey and the establishment of new excavations, on Kuyunjik and at various points
in the northern half of the lower town during the initial 1987 season, we began work at the Halzi
Gate on 2 April 1989. Our point of departure was from a still-visible opening in the outer, lower
curtain wall at the apparent mid-point of the east face of the gate. Here we exposed the surviving
south jamb of a stone doorway, of which only two courses remained. The inner leading edge of
the two small finely worked stones is not vertical and slants slightly inwards (Fig. 3).' The removal
of the earth in the central area to the north of this slanting stone included backfill from a trench
cut by Mahdloum.9

A courtyard protected by apotropaic spirits1 2
The lower part of the wall of the court was lined with undecorated limestone orthostats. The
tops of several of these slabs were already exposed in the south-eastern front corner of the outer
court in square U9. In this disturbed area the foundation box was already damaged and the
prophylactic figure that had been placed there was missing.
On the once hidden reverse face of an orthostat at the southeast corner of the court, the work
exposed one of Sennacherib's standard building inscriptions (Fig. 22), which read,
Sennacherib, king of the land Assur, built anew the wall of Nineveh."3
Following the varying width orthostats that lined the south wall of the court, and cutting deep
through collapsed upper wall debris, we reached the corner in square R9. Excavating down to the
base of the last orthostat, we came upon an intact foundation box constructed of parts of clay
bricks, situated immediately below the level of the floor (Fig. 23). The large court measured
45x 19m.
The apotropaic male human-hero figure placed in the box is made of lightly-fired pale buff clay,
with a fugitive sky-blue powder covering the whole figure, and not only the garment14 (Fig. 24).
The demeanour and face of the figure Lulal (see fn. 15) is gentle and non-aggressive. The figure's
long hair is arranged in bands and a square beard is modelled similarly. He wears a long, unbelted
tunic with elbow-length sleeves. Four vertical lines at the base of the figure may indicate feet. The
figure stands in a frontal pose with arms bent at the elbow; in the right hand he holds a mace (or
crook) and in the left an axe. As it was situated in the rear (south west) corner of the court
protecting the inner gate, it is logical to suppose that a duplicate of this statue is still located in
the other rear (northwest) corner in square RIOO.

corner of the northeastern end of the court, a 2 x 2 m trench was laid out and that corner defined.

The single skeleton (A) lying behind the horse skeleton, the most easterly of all of the human
skeletons, is an adult male of whom only the calvarium and part of the humerus were recovered.
Skeleton 1 lies sprawled face downwards (Fig. 29). He is an adult male 174 cm tall, 30 to 35 years
old. His injuries included six broken ribs, a damaged incompletely broken radius and ulna of the
right arm, and broken fingers of the left hand. Associated body stress in the right elbow and ball
of the left foot suggests habitual kneeling; implying that he was an archer (Bamett ND: 11 ).2
Skeleton 2 is that of a pre-adolescent male, 12 to 13 years of age, small for his age (Figs. 29-30).
The skeleton was in poor condition. There are knife wounds above the eye and a fractured skull.
The younger individuals appear to have suffered from childhood diseases, not seen in the older
individuals, perhaps reflecting a declining economy with consequent poor nutrition.
Beneath skeleton 3 was a fragmentary socketed iron spear (see Fig. 1) and next to the femur,
a bronze trilobate arrowhead. The body is that of an adult male 22 to 24 years old, 180 cm tall.
There was a crushing fracture of the fifth lumbar vertebra. Lumbar lesions were attributed to
brucellosis, a bacterial infection derived from goat milk products.
Skeleton 4 (Fig. 31) is a pre-adolescent, 11 to 12 years old, tall for his age. A copper/bronze
earring was associated with the left ear (NIN MA 90/110). There was a bronze trilobate arrow
embedded in the left fibula, and another below the right wrist, a total of six arrowheads were
found in the vicinity of this individual. A bone tube was excavated from below the body. The
crush of people in the corridor was overlaid with fallen bricks and burnt beams, suggesting that
there were multiple causes leading to the death of these individuals, some of whom were crushed
by falling debris.
Individual 5 is an adult male 35 to 40 years of age, 172 cm tall; this was possibly the oldest
individual, evinced by both arthritis and periodontal disease. There were stab wounds in the back
and through the lower right ribcage, and both legs were fractured. Functional stress on the upper
right arm indicates he was an archer. There are numerous old, healed injuries, indicating that this
was a seasoned warrior.
Individual 6 is an adolescent male aged 17 to 18 years. The lower spine is damaged from heavy
stress. There is a fracture of the left rib near the sternum. A silver earring, with a composite design
in the form of an inverted crescent suspended below a disc, lay beneath the left wrist. A pale-blue
chalcedony conoid stamp seal and attached bronze elbow fibula (NIN 90/68) lay underneath the
pelvis and left leg, and two bronze arrows were also found near the body (Figs. 40-2).
Individual 7 (Fig. 32) is an adult male 35 years old, 168 cm tall. There are cut-marks on the
left side of the skull, and he is sprawled face down on the pavement. He shows stress of the lower
back and shoulders and in the ball of the right foot, suggesting that he was an archer. There are
several old, healed wounds to the body. Underneath the left hip was a partial bronze fibula (NIN
MA 90/102), and an earring (NIN 90/47) lay near the temporomandibular joint.
A group of skeletons numbered 8, 9, 10 and 11 represents a cluster of younger people, who
were located towards the rear of the entrance passage in a less dense configuration. The group
includes one child 7 to 8 years old (skeleton 8); a silver earring was found near the right ear, and
an associated bronze fibula (NIN 90/63) was excavated beneath the right humerus. The position
of the skeleton suggests that the back was broken. An 11 to 12-year-old individual (skeleton 9)
had arms burnt by falling debris. An infant of 10 months (skeleton 10) and a child 3 years old
(skeleton 11) were the youngest to die in the Halzi Gate conflagration.
Individual 12 (Fig. 33) is an adult male 27 to 30 years of age, 166 cm tall. There are cuts in the
skull and a penetrating wound through the lower right abdomen caused by a blunt weapon which
left a lesion in the right pelvic bone. A repetitive cupping and twisting motion of the right hand,
resulting in arthritis with a stress-related injury of the right shoulder, suggests military duty. An
iron object is lying beneath the left shoulder (Fig. 34). In total the weaponry scattered amongst
the skeletons included twenty bronze arrowheads, a lancehead, and iron spears. Four of the bronze
arrowheads were bifacial, three transitional-trilobate and the remainder trilobite (Fig. 35).

Concluding remarks
The evidence at the gate illustrates the fierce conflagration and the combat between the Assyrians
and a combined enemy force of Medes and Babylonians in 612 BC. Tentative plans to record DNA
data from the skeletons had to be abandoned due to their poor preservation and contamination
risk. Further detailed cranial and dental examination and X-rays have been carried out.
The excavation was halted abruptly by political circumstances and further excavation at the
Halzi Gate remains desirable in order to complete the gate plan. Nineveh (Fig. 43) is both a
challenging and rewarding site at which to excavate and we could not have accomplished as much
as we did without the support of numerous Iraqi colleagues, to whom we owe a great debt of
