Despite somne excellent treatments of the Old Babylonian Eduba in the
last few years,' the question of how Sumerian was learned has been rather
neglected. Few attempts at an answer have ever been made, and none go
beyond speculation-indeed, the question itself was seldom raised.2 Yet it
is surely an important one. We know that the literature used in the schools
was almost exclusively Sumerian; that Sumerian also served as the
vehicular language for other disciplines; that examinations were largely
held in Sumerian-in short, that (upiarraiitu was in fact a specialized
schooling in Sumerian as well as a comprehensive schooling through

1. The literary sources seem to be silent on the matter of how Sumerian
was taught. Even extreme measures, such as taking seriously the school
essays and disputations, would not enlighten us as to practical details; these
texts were never meant to provide this kind of information. It might be
objected that we do have the "First Steps in Sumerian" in the multitude of
lexical texts of all descriptions.4 I doubt it. First of all, there are the sign lists
(syllabaries and cognates), whose object was a training in the correct
formation of the signs and their correct use as to their phonetic and
logographic function. In other words, the goal was to learn mihiltum (gii-
sum, "writing"), not Sumerian morphology and syntax.
Secondly, the word lists (lexical or, perhaps better, encyclopaedic by
nature), whether of the general or of the specialized type (for example, ur5-
ra=-hubullu and hi=--a, respectively), are undoubtedly very useful in
broadening and ordering one's command of Sumerian vocabulary; but
they are hardly any help in trying to put together even the simplest
Sumerian sentence.
Lastly, there are some works that are undoubtedly textbooks. But here
again it is difficult to see how they could be used for the initial steps in
Sumerian. ana ittilu is indeed a splendidly conceived textbook of legal
terminology in both Akkadian and Sumerian; but "legalese," not Sumerian,
is the object.5 The grammatical texts-and for our purpose only the Old
Babylonian Grammatical Texts may legitimately be used-are fascinating
material, as may be illustrated by the many modern studies of the finer
points of Sumerian grammar that result from these comparative
literary texts (p. 131), but he didn't draw any inference from this observation, and nowhere
mentioned any language teaching method.
In his masterly treatment Teachers and Students in the Oldest Schools (London, 1956),
Gadd touched only upon the use of lists of personal names and of signs as "staples of
instruction" (pp. 11-12), but he clearly meant instruction in writing. I fail to see how these
aids could constitute "a means of instruction . .. in both of the literary languages" (p. 12).
H. W. F. Saggs does not mention the problem in his treatment of education or schools (The
Greatness that was Babylon [London, 1962] 187-89 and 434-38).
In short, almost everybody tacitly assumes that using bilingual lists and paradigms was
sufficient to learn Sumerian, although W. W. Hallo rightly states that "Sumerian syntax could
hardly be learned except from connected prose and poetry" (The Ancient Near East [New
York, 1971] p. 157). It is therefore somewhat bewildering that, having pointed out the
popularity of proverbs in the curriculum, he goes on to say that they "can better be described
in the context not of scribal education but of religious literature and of higher learning" (p.
158).

2. One other avenue must be explored: the vast number of school copies
of literary texts. We have long known that literature in the Old Babylonian
period was mainly, if not exclusively, an Eduba affair. But the implications
of this observation have not always been explicitly stated. We must note
from the start that the majority of these literary texts can hardly have
functioned in first year Sumerian: they are often far too difficult, and the
copies we possess are often of a very high quality, at least in Nippur, even
when they are exercises and not library copies. Especially in the case of the
major compositions, one has the feeling that the object was mainly scribal
edification and the preservation of Sumerian literature as such. The
educational value will have been humanistic; by that I mean in this context
that the texts were read and memorized for their content and literary
qualities, while the copying was done for the preservation of literature, in
that the extract copies are exercises in correct and beautiful copying.

3. The opinion I expressed in my edition of "Lipit-EIstar's Praise" (JCS 30

corresponding noun phrases. Even without a detailed line-by-line analysis,
an unexpected variety of different forms emerges.
(a) In lines 1-12 we find only verbal or semi-verbal "simple" forms, that
is, forms without affixes. This in itself is rather strange, but a more detailed
examination allows us to subdivide this string into smaller units. Thus we
have in lines 1-2 "participial" forms of compound verbs (sag-il and b6-du7)
used almost as Akkadian adjectives (kabfu!); note also that sag-il seems to
be an epithet of lugal, preceding a genitive construction (nun bira-ga),

Among the twenty-odd manuscripts there are only two that are not
obviously first stage exercises: the Louvre Prism and the Philadelphia
tablet. The rest are: (a) large tablets originally containing the complete
texts, but clumsily written; some of them have lexical exercises on the rest
of the tablet (for example, text F); (b) extract tablets of varying length; five
of these are typically shaped: oblong, with square corners and straight
sides, and thicker than normal "literary" tablets (for example, C, G, I, J);
some of these also show lexical exercises; (c) finally, no less than nine school
"buns," representing about one line in six of the complete text-a very high
percentage (the Sx texts).
Furthermore, in many manuscripts the lines are subdivided in such a way
that the natural division of the sentences into noun phrase and verb phrase
becomes clear-a system that is far from widespread. Lastly, although I do
not think this is very important in itself, the text is nowhere mentioned in a
catalogue, although its pendant (lugal-miduga'4) is twice so mentioned.
This could mean that our text, by its nature as a "first year exercise text,"
was not deemed worthy of inclusion in the official literary curriculum.
I should perhaps stress the fact that this peculiar distribution of
manuscripts, which is crucial to the argument for the composition's
pedagogical function, became clear only after the eight "advanced"
Philadelphia fragments had been joined to form one complete tablet. This
is a strong argument in favor of my contention'5 that much more
importance should be attached to autopsy of tablets in general and to
joining in particular.

Eduba" is atypical in that so much emphasis is laid on an aspect that
concurs with the external form of the actual tablets as well as with the
presumed reason for its peculiar grammatical construction. It is perhaps
not too hazardous to interpret this as having again an educational purpose.
After all, it is being made quite clear that this king's fame will endure by
virtue of his talents as a scribe, and how, in his case, and therefore perhaps
also in the case of all students, scribal training opens up many possibilities:
acquisition of wisdom, enjoyment of writing and literature, important jobs,
and so on. Finally, the closing passage may well be intended as a self-
fulfilling prophecy: of course his fame will endure when this text will
remain in use for many centuries (with some interruption, just about forty
by now) as a school text.

student the following elements of the language: (a) the signs with their
reading and meaning; this is of course omnipresent and obvious in any
school text; (b) some basic features of the Sumerian verbal system: the
three main types of predicate, with their subtypes; the rather strange
preponderance of simple forms (that is, without affixes) may have
something to do with the parallel or perhaps immediately preceding
"study" of the lexical texts: verbal stems do not normally occur there in
inflected form; (c) quite a number of sentence patterns or nominal patterns

placed near each other, leading to a better understanding of the structure as
a whole as well as its possibilities in various contexts; moreover, in that it is
highly probable that at some level the Akkadian equivalents, where
applicable, were contrasted orally with the Sumerian structures and
expressions; (e) sentence-bound, in that the structures corresponding to the
parts of speech are not isolated, but are always used and contrasted at the
sentence level, and in that quite some different sentence patterns are
introduced; (f) progressive, in that there is a gradual movement from
simpler patterns towards more complicated ones (from simple forms to
copulative constructions and inflected forms).
This, then, is perhaps how Sumerian was taught. Any conclusions as to
the educational values of this method, and perhaps its modernity (although
this may well be deemed irrelevant) are gladly left to the reader.

These proverbs, with their succinct style and variegated diction and
grammar, are excellent teaching material. In this connection we may well

C. If the interpretation offered in these pages is correct, there are some
important implications. Guided by this text and the proverbs, we may yet
discover other first grade textbooks. It goes without saying that this can
only by done by autopsy. The test will be fourfold: the manuscripts will
show a very high percentage of "early" exercises, indicated by shape and
writing; grammatical structure and content will be comparable; they will
not be very long; and there will be allusions to scholarly activity. Although
I am not sure that the last criterion is necessary, I am inclined to believe
that we shall find at least that such schoolbooks will not be in the
mainstream of any given genre. It will indeed be fascinating to examine
which genres-if any-will be represented on this level.
It seems not impossible that in this way we may arrive at a much more
detailed reconstruction of scribal education, and that, always referring to
the shape of the tablets and the form of the script, we may recognize
various stages. I suspect that we shall find a first stage consisting mainly of
syllabaries, extracts of lexical lists, proverbs, and shorter texts not unlike
the text treated here; a second stage showing more difficult or longer
extracts from the lexical lists, longer literary compositions including, I
think, most of the school essays and disputations; the third stage will then
be the higher, "academic," stage, where literature is studied and copied
for its own sake, and where probably specialized disciplines of learning
branch off progressively. But all this is mere speculation.
