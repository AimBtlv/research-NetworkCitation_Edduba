RASSAM’S ACTIVITIES AT TELLO (1879)
AND THE EARLIEST ACQUISITION
OF NEO-SUMERIAN TABLETS
IN THE BRITISH MUSEUM
Lorenzo Verderame (Università di Palermo)
The present contribution is dedicated to Marcel Sigrist, who has spent many years working in the
Students Room of the Department of the Middle East at the British Museum cataloging and editing
Neo-Sumerian texts, particularly the thousands of fragments that ˜ll the storerooms of museums. This
is an exhausting task that continues to be burdened by the ˜rst impression of these documents that led
H. Pognon to describe them as “laundry lists.”1 This paper, which starts from one of those small frag-
ments, wishes to show how this ˜eld of research is a source of great surprise and emotion for those few
people who venture to study it.
The ˜rst part is written in the ˜rst person, and is meant to be a diary of the usual activities carried
out at the British Museum during the past years. It aims to be a document that on the one hand can be
used as a source for future historiographic research, and on the other hand gives a more human per-
spective on our work.2
Neo-Sumerian Texts from “Sippar”?
In 2005 I spent a six months doing research in the Students Room, working on the catalog and tran-
scribing Ur III documents.3 This was a long stretch of research, and going through the collections of the
Museum, I began to take an interest in the organization of such collections and in their structure,
assisted and spurred on by C. B. F. Walker who, at the time, was deputy keeper of the department.
I am deeply indebted to Christopher Walker, who proposed the topic of this paper, produced documents from the British
Museum archives, and read the manuscript. All the pictures were taken by me and published, together with the texts, with the
permission of the Trustees of the British Museum.

Rassam’s Excavations at Sippar
The provenance of the fragments that Walker was registering was Abu Habba, the Arabic name
for Sippar, excavated by Rassam from January 1881 to October 1882.16 We know very little about
the collections sent by Rassam to the British Museum. The details of his activities on the site are scarce
because his occasional reports to the Museum lack information about the progress of the excavations.17
Rassam was accustomed to leaving the responsibilities of the digging to reliable foremen while he
went on to visit other sites in the region.18 Furthermore, he was absent from Sippar for a long period,
from 3 May 1881 to 2 May 1882, while his agents supervised the work and reported the results directly
to him.19 Ward visited the excavations at Sippar and witnessed the excavation techniques adopted by
Rassam: “The men who conducted the excavations for Mr. Rassam showed us all about . . . and assured
us that they knew, by the indications of the ashes, where further tablets could be found by a day or two’s
digging.”20 The men who worked with Rassam at Sippar were also active in Baghdad, as Ward states.21
Rassam gathered materials from his work at various sites,22 which he stored together with older lots,23
and later shipped together to London. At the British Museum, the objects were cataloged according to

The Earliest Acquisition of Neo-Sumerian Objects in European Collections
Considering that these tablets come from Girsu, and having in mind the pattern of how Rassam’s
collections were treated at the British Museum, it is now time to ask how Rassam might have acquired
them. Our research starts with the third millennium inscribed objects discovered or acquired by Euro-
pean museums before Rassam’s excavations.30
Since most of these objects were kept in private collections, and were often published in antiquarian
or non-specialized journals without any indication of their provenance, it is di¯cult, and often impos-
sible, to trace the story of some of these objects. Materials deriving from an excavation, that is those
that were gathered directly in situ, are scarce. The visit to Kis by Oppert in 1852 led to the discovery

Another reason that permission from Constantinople was not strictly necessary was that Tello was
located in the area of the Munte˜k tribe, which at the time was integrated in a sort of independent
state under Nâsir Pasha, the builder of Nasirîya.66 De Sarzec contacted the Pasha and was able to get
permission to travel around and excavate any mound in the area.67
In the summer of 1878, de Sarzec returned to France,68 bringing with him the results of the exca-
vations that were then exhibited at the Louvre. The museum purchased the pieces, in part with funds
provided by the French parliament (Chambres).69 De Sarzec then returned to Tello armed with a
faramân from Constantinople and, with a grant from his government, conducted the “o¯cial” excava-
tion70 of the site the 21 January 1880 until the winter of 1881.71 Between 1881 and 1891 de Sarzec
returned frequently to Tello to dig one of the mounds, but the long intervals between expeditions, and
the absence of any guards at the site, provided locals with opportunities to search for tablet archives.72
The illicit excavations began as early as 1894,73 the same year that Léon Heuzey publicly presented the
results of the eight campaigns in Tello, including roughly thirty thousand documents from the third

Conclusions
What then of our Ur III texts? Tentatively, we may suggest that they represent a surface ˜nd picked
up by Rassam on the site where de Sarzec and the dealers subsequently found the archives of Girsu.92
This may support the idea that it was the dealers who found the archive before de Sarzec.
This is only an hypothetical reconstruction, but looking at history in a romantic view, we prefer to
imagine that we have dealt with objects, booty of the Rassam’s activity in Tello, which have been
among the ˜rst Ur III tablets to be recovered in Mesopotamia.
