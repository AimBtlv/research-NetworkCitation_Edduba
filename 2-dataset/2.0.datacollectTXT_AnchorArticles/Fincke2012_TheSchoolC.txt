1. GENERAL INTRODUCTION
The three Ancient Near Eastern cities that are the subject of this study – Hattuša, the capital of the
˘
Hittite kingdom, located in present-day Anatolia, Ugarit, a city-state on the Mediterranean coast of
Syria, and Emar, located on the river Euphrates in northern Syria (see the map on page VI) – have
the following features in common. All three cities are located on the western or north-western
periphery of Mesopotamia and they all produced ‘school texts’ from the Late Bronze Age, which
means from the mid-second millennium BC until roughly 1200 BC. The spoken language in each
place differed not only significantly from the others but also from the lingua franca of those days,
Akkadian. The same holds true for the scripts and materials they used to write their native
languages. This situation led to the need for providing a local school education according to the
Mesopotamian model in order to teach Akkadian in each place adequately. Since the students of
each school faced the same problem of learning a language that was not only different from their
native one, but also sometimes even belonged to a different language family, it is of particular
interest to compare the school curricula of all these places.
2. ‘SCHOOL EDUCATION’ AND ‘SCHOOL CURRICULUM’ IN THE CONTEXT OF THIS STUDY
‘School education’ and ‘school curriculum’ in connection with the Late Bronze Age cities on the
periphery of Mesopotamia mean dealing with learning Sumerian and Akkadian and reading
compositions of Mesopotamian origin written in both languages on cuneiform tablets. The school
curriculum used for this purpose was derived from the Old Babylonian one (ca. 19th-16th century BC)
and differs substantially from the curriculum used to teach the local language in the places in
question.1 For the students in Hattuša, Emar, and Ugarit, school education according to the

The school curriculum these three Late Bronze Age cities used to learn Sumerian and
Akkadian derived from the Mesopotamian one and accordingly consisted of two parts. During the
first phase the pupils learned the cuneiform signs and wrote endless lists of words and compound
words as well as short grammatical exercises, all of them monolingually in either Sumerian or
Akkadian or bilingually in both languages. Occasionally, also a column in their own native
language was added. The objective of the second phase of the school education was to acquire and
master both languages by copying sequences of proverbs, narratives, letters, religious texts and the
like. The students also learned how to compose a colophon, which was meant to give information
about the name of the composition, about the scribe himself – his name and perhaps his profession
or rank within the apprenticeship to become a scribe – and the purpose for which the tablets were
written. The second phase of the school curriculum is the subject of the following study.
3. ‘SCHOOL TEXTS’ IN THE CONTEXT OF THIS STUDY
In order to reconstruct the school curriculum of each city, the school texts need to be studied. The
school curriculum of the first phase of the school education can easily be reconstructed with help of
the pupils’ school exercise tablets, because they have a distinct, inexperienced handwriting.3 When
the students entered the second phase of their school education, their handwriting had become very
elaborate; at first glance, the tablets of students do not differ from the ones written by fully
educated scribes. The only possible way to learn about the qualification of the writer would be the
colophon. Unfortunately, the relevant tablets from Hattuša have generally no colophon. There is
˘
only one exception: one tablet from Hattuša can be clearly identified as a school exercise text,
˘
because the writer explained in his colophon that he had written down an exercise given by his
teacher.4 The text in question is a medical text, a text genre that is not common among the
Mesopotamian school texts.
The situation in the other two cities in question is slightly different. The school texts from
Emar5 – all of them have been found at the same place, in the so-called Temple M1 (see § 5) –
occasionally have a colophon. Among the Ugarit school texts6 only some of the ones found at the
Maison aux Tablettes Littéraires have one. All the other texts could be either school exercise

6. THE SECOND PHASE SCHOOL TEXTS FROM UGARIT
In Ugarit, which is located on the Syrian coast of the Mediterranean Sea, school texts18 were found
in various houses within the city.19 These houses belonged to officials working for the city
administration.20 The attestation of school texts in these places reveals that these officials educated
pupils and students in their private houses and in so doing ran a kind of private ‘school’ in addition
to their official duties.21 Most of these private schools in Ugarit seemed to have specialised in
teaching certain text genres that cover more or less parts of the curriculum.22 This means that the
students must have gone to different ‘schools’ depending on the lections they needed to learn. The
school curriculum of the second phase of school education in Ugarit was comprised of the
following five groups (Figure 4).

9. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
The corpus of second phase school texts varies in large number between the cities in question. The
total is 388 (Hattuša) : 130 (Emar) : 62 (Ugarit) (see § 7), which makes a ratio of about 6 : 2 : 1.
˘
Consequently, the corpus from Hattuša has the largest variety, and many overlaps between two
˘
cities involve Hattuša.
˘
When it comes to assessing the five groups of school texts (see above, § 7 and Figure 5), the largest
number of them in both Hattuša and Emar is in the group of divination texts, and next to that in the
˘
98 J.C. FINCKE
group of religious texts.39 This prominence of religious texts in Hattuša mirrors the number of
˘
Hittite tablets unearthed in the Hittite capital, which are generally said to form about 80 %.
Religion clearly played an immense part in Hittite society, which had an effect also on the
cuneiform school curriculum. The emphasis on these groups of texts in the Emar curriculum can be
interpreted in the light of the find-spot of the school texts: the so-called Temple M1, which also
housed the archive of a priest, and seems to have run a school for priests and temple personnel (see
above, § 7).
In Ugarit, the focus is clearly on the group of ‘text genres known from the Old Babylonian
school curriculum’. The secondary group is made up of the religious and divinatory texts. All three
groups together form more than 90 % of the school curriculum, which does not leave much room
for other text genres. This would suggest that the curriculum of Ugarit was much closer to the
Mesopotamian model than to the curriculum of the other two cities.
Medical texts, which form quite a large group in Hattuša (14.43 % or the third largest
˘
group), play almost no role in Emar (0.77 %) and Ugarit (8,07 %).
So far, only two groups of school texts produce individual compositions that are attested in more
than one city (see above, § 8): the ‘text genres known from the Old Babylonian school curriculum’
(7 compositions, hereafter marked with ‘a’) and divination (one composition, hereafter marked
with ‘b’). Only one composition is part of all three school curricula, albeit in different versions: the
Gilgameš-epic (group a).
Contrary to the similar emphasis on the groups of school texts in Hattuša and Emar, there is
˘
almost no similarity between these two cities when it comes to the overlapping of individual
compositions. Only one composition from the group of divination (b) has been found in both
Hattuša and Emar.
˘
Although there is not much similarity in the general emphasis on the groups of school texts
between Hattuša and Ugarit apart from the religious texts (in both, incantations have the largest
˘
number followed by hymns; prayers are only attested in these two cities, not in Emar; see Figure 7),
three compositions are attested only at these two places (all from group a). The copy of one of them
that was written in Hattuša even ended up in Ugarit, but was never copied again in Syria.
˘
There are only three compositions that are attested in both Ugarit and Emar (group a) but
not in Hattuša. Also for two text genres from the group of ‘text genres known from the Old
˘
Babylonian school curriculum’ an overlap between both cities can be found: fables are only
attested in Emar and Ugarit, but the so-called edubba-texts are absent in both cities (see Figure 6).
The results of this study can be explained in the light of the political and geographical situation.
Considering the political circumstances in Syria during the Late Bronze Age, the given results are
easy to explain: during the thirteenth and twelfth century BC, Emar was subject to the king of
Carchemiš, who was a Hittite client-king.40 The political influence from Hattuša not only left traces
˘
39 This is the case if one neglects the group of unidentified and unclassified fragments only attested
in Hattuša.
˘
40 See e.g. Yamada 2006.

˘
in the ductus of the cuneiform script,41 but obviously also in the Emar school curriculum. This
influence, however, did not necessarily result in the exchange of school texts, scholars or teachers,
but in the general focus of the school curriculum.
In Ugarit, the situation was different: from the thirteenth century onwards, Ugarit had to
pay tribute to the Hittite king, but remained independent with its own local kings and culture,
which can also be seen in the general outline of the school curriculum. Nevertheless, the alliance
between both cities led to an exchange of school texts and probably also of scholars.
Regardless of the political situation, Emar and Ugarit were both Syrian cities. Therefore,
both cities belonged to the same cultural sphere.42 Even as regards the transmission of foreign
literature, Emar and Ugarit were closer together than any of these cities and Hattuša. One of the
˘
routes from Babylonia to the Hittite kingdom ran via Syria. Scholars who took this route passed
through Syria; some of them even stayed. It is therefore not surprising to find compositions attested
in Emar and Ugarit, but not in Hattuša.
