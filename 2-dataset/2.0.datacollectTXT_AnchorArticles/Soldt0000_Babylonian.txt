# TEXT



The ancient city of Ugarit has provided us with a wealth of textual material. A large portion of this material is made up by lexical, religious and literary texts. The lexical texts can be unilingual (usually Sumerian) or multilingual (usually Sumerian with an Akkadian translation) ', the religious and literary texts can be in Sumerian or in Akkadian, very seldom are they. bilingual. 1 will discuss the lexical texts and the religious and literary texts as two separate groups. After that, we will look at their common characteristics.

1. The lexical texts

Tell Ras Shamra has been a constant rich source of lexical material. Since 1929, hundreds of texts have come to light and with every new campaign new texts are added to the corpus. ? As we know from Mesopotamia, the main purpose. of these lexical texts was to provide apprentice scribes with the necessary study material in order to learn the script as well as the languages written with it. However, the students at Ugarit found themselves in a position even less enviable than their Mesopotamian colleagues. Ever since the Old Babylonian period Sumerian was for the students a language that had to be acquired by learning and this gave rise to the addition of an Akkadian translation in most of the lexical texts. After the Old Babylonian period these bilingual texts kept being used as study material and the peripheral sites, which are by far our most important source for lexical material in the Late Bronze Age, ate no exception. However, unlike their Mesopotamian colleagues, the scribes in Ugarit not only had ‘to study Sumerian, which for them had become a mere collection of logograms rather than a written language, but also Akkadian, the language used as lingua franca in the West during this period. a. The curriculum

The lexical texts were found in large concentrations scattered over the city. These concentrations occur in and around buildings which supposedly served as archives. A number of houses have been identified and some of them are now referred to by the names of their assumed owners, usually a person who appears in a prominent position in the legal and administrative documents found in the house. Thus, we speak of the houses of RaSap’abu, Rap’anu, Yabninu, etc. However, in most cases we are unable to make out who the owner of the house was and this has given rise to anonymous descriptions such as "The house of the Lettré, of the High Priest, of the Hurrian Priest", etc.

b. Different types of tablets.

The predominant type among the many lexical texts is the one which gives a complete unilingual text of one particular tablet from a series. Of this type, we often find several duplicates within individual archives (see fig. 2, p. 194), and from this we can conclude that several students probably worked either side by side or in consecutive classes, Especially the last point, the chronological distribution of the tablets, needs clearing up. Were the texts written within a Short span of time or do we have to assume that older texts were kept for future reference? J have tried to show elsewhere that texts of ephemeral importance were as a rule not kept on file for a long time and the lexical texts would certainly qualify as ‘ephemeral'”. As can be seen from Appendix III almost all more or less datable texts were apparently written within the last fifty years of Ugarit's existence, It is only the scribe Ba‘lasku who perhaps wrote his text during the reign of Niqmepa‘, provided that his teacher SapSu-malku was indeed the scribe of the same name who appears in legal documents 7. Thus, with the exception of a few texts, it seems likely that the bulk of lexical tablets from Ugarit dates to the period immediately preceding the destruction of the city. It is, however, by no means impossible. that some tablets were kept for use in school education or as copies for personal use by the scribes once they had finished their study. For the latter assertion we have, however, no clear evidence. In any case, the destruction of Ugarit has given us a random indication of the contents of a school. Apparently, unilingual versions were the preferred format.

The two remaining categories are the bilingual texts and the excerpts. The bilingual texts are attested for every type of text which allows a translation column, and for every archive. Certain types, however, show a clear preference for bilingual texts: the Grammatical Texts (RSGT, no uniligual texts) and Izi and Diri. c. Other texts.

The lexical texts discussed so far are not the only exercises which the scribes wrote for their training. Apart from the many literary texts, which I will discuss below, they occasionally also wrote acrographic lists of personal names. The lists were written on separate tablets and were not part of a fixed repertoire, but were perhaps made up locally: they usually have a selection of indigenous names. * The same type of practice texts can be found among the alphabetic exercise tablets, to which I will come back below. An exercise tablet with a legal text is perhaps 16.180 (PRU 3, 41). The text appears to have an alphabet (collated) at the end. However, there are traces of seals on the obverse, which are unexpected in the case of an exercise.

2. The religious and literary texts .

The religious and literary texts from RasShamta are remarkable both in number and variety. The altogether ca, 40 texts. range from incantations to astrological omens and from hemerologies to fables and_wisdom texts. Most of these texts were published in Ugaritica 5 by Nougayrol, a few remain unpublished, When the texts appeared, parallels could not immediately be found for all of them and this gave rise to the idea that ‘the literature contained in them was at least to some extent the result. of the creative effort of the Ugaritic scribes themselves. > However, as time went by, more and more parallels to the texts from Ugarit turned up and, especially after the publication of the texts found at Emar, there are hardly texts left without parallels. An overview of all the material is given in Appendix II.

b. The raison d’étre of the texts

In view of the relatively large number of religious and literary texts one has to pose the question why the scribes copied these texts.. The many parallels from Mesopotamia and other peripheral sites show that we are not dealing here with original compositions. Apparently the texts had been imported for a specific purpose, which becomes clear when we take the findspots of the tablets into account, As it tums out, practically all the religious and literary texts were found in the archives which also housed the lexical texts: they. were in fact part of the same hoards of tablets. Moreover, the level of proficiency of the scribes who wrote them was rather low, as can be seen from the many mistakes that they made. This is also true for the incantations, texts which might have been used by priests to conjure evil. However, these, too, have not been found in the houses of priests (like the Hurrian Priest or the High Priest), but likewise among

—_—— 5. The alphabetic texts

a, Study material

The Ugaritic alphabet consisted of just thirty signs and was — at least in comparison to the syilabic script — relatively easy to master. The scribes who were trained to write texts in alphabetic script had to write two types of exercises: the alphabet and practice texts (fig. 3, p. 195). The student began by copying the alphabet. We have one exercise in which the master first wrote his version, which was then copied by the student (KTU 5.20). He then went on to practice the alphabet in context and for that purpose he. wrote lists of personal names (which are sometimes acrographic), exercise letters and even parts of religious and literary texts. Interesting is the alphabet with syllabic ‘equations’, KTU 5.14 ®. These exercises served as an introduction to the types of text that the future scribe would have to write: administrative lists, letters, religious and literary texts. In short, they give a good picture ofthe most important categories distinguished in KTU (1, 2 & 4).

Thus, the material used for the study of the alphabetic and syllabic scripts was more.or less organized into a curriculum, first the list of signs, then practice texts. Most striking are the acrographic lists of personal names on separate practice tablets in both syllabic and alphabetic script. This leads us to the question whether scribes were able to read and write both scripts (and understand the languages written with them), or confined themselves to one script only.

b. The use of the alphabetic and syllabic scripts by the same scribe

The first question that must be answered is: did scribes who were trained in writing the syllabic script also leam the alphabetic script, or was there a complete separation? The latter does not seem very likely. After all, a scribe who was able to master the complicated syllabic cuneiform would certainly have no difficulty with an alphabet of just thirty signs with which he could write his own language. The Significance of SPN in West Semitic Thought

A contribution to the history of a mythological motif Nicholas Wyatt - Edinburgh

A paper on the theme of the History of Religions and Ugaritic myth could lead in a number of directions. Those that are entirely theoretical, in the sense that they would deal with problems in the theoretical approach to the discipline, are to my mind not terribly helpful, since grand theory always has to give way to awkward fact. The problem I shall treat is no less theoretical, of course, but at least it is testable, and seeks to answer a specific, and ultimately historical, problem, rather than simply indulging in airy speculation, or more dangerously, in the promotion of an ideological stance. The “History of Religions" indeed raises a problem of reference: the English expression is fairly neutral, simply

_ denoting the study of religions within a historical framework, considering

historical developments and so forth, while as I understand it the German term Religionsgeschichte can have a more restricted sense, denoting this approach applied to Israelite and Judahite religion in historical context, so that it merges with Comparative Religion and the History of Tradition (Uberlieferungsgeschichte). It can then take on a significant polemical role, which I do not see it as my brief to defend ',

The "History of Religions" is better pursued in the former sense, but at the same time it is preferable to approach it ad textum or ad contextum rather than in the abstract, and the problems associated with spn seem to me an excellent field for enquiry in this context. Inevitably, since this topic also impinges on biblical matters, it becomes a study bridging the divide noted above.

There has been considerable discussion conceming the. etymology and meaning of the Ugaritic term spn, appearing in Hebrew as sdpdn ("Saphon"). The vocalisation problem, which has taken up an inordinate amount of discussion, may be summed. up as follows:

i) the unvocalised Ugaritic form is spx. ii) in Akkadian it is normally transcribed as Sepunu (nominative) as in sa-panmim™ (TM.75.G.1642) 7, or sapuna (oblique), as in the form gir sa-pu1 For a useful treatment of the problem, including problems of translation, see Hahn 1956, 83-118. .
