piotr michalowski and niek veldhuis (eds):
Approaches to Sumerian Literature. Studies in Honour of S tip (H. L.
J. Vanstiphout).
(Cuneiform Monographs.) vi, 247 pp. Leiden and Boston: Brill,
2006. €90. ISBN 9004 15325 X.
This volume holds a collection of essays written to celebrate the scholarly
career of H. L. J. Vanstiphout. The essays are bound by a common theme, the
study of Sumerian literature, the field to which Vanstiphout devoted so much
of his energy. During the course of his career Vanstiphout assembled around
him a select but eclectic group of students and colleagues who met periodically
in Groningen to discuss ancient Mesopotamian literature and to bring to its
study modern methodologies in literary criticism. Several of the contributors to
this volume are veterans of the Groningen meetings.
Bendt Alster ("Ninurta and the Turtle: on parodia sacra in Sumerian
literature", pp. 13-36) returns to an enigmatic literary composition of which he
made the first full edition in 1972. Nicole Brisch ("In praise of the kings of
Larsa", pp. 37-45) examines the evolution of the genre of royal praise poetry in
the period of its last flowering. A. J. Ferrara ("Some thoughts on narrative
now and then", pp. 47-66) subjects to higher literary criticism the cosmogonic
narratives that open Sumerian literary compositions. Alhena Gadotti
("Gilgameš, Gudam, and the singer in Sumerian literature", pp. 67-83) is a
study in intertextuality. W. W. Hallo ("A Sumerian apocryphon? The royal
correspondence of Ur reconsidered", pp. 85-104) is a response to a recent
sceptical assessment of the historical value of the genre of Sumerian royal
letters. Dina Katz ("Appeals to Utu in Sumerian narratives", pp. 105-22)
examines a recurrent literary motif in which a doomed character appeals to
the sun god for help. Jacob Klein ("Man and his God: a wisdom poem or a
cultic lament", pp. 123-43) studies a difficult Sumerian poem from the point
of view of genre and form. Piotr Michalowski ("The strange history of
Tummal", pp. 145-65) brings an important new insight to the Tummal
composition and rejects it as a text of any historical value. Gonzalo Rubio

("Šulgi and the death of Sumerian", pp. 167-79) considers king Šulgi's claims
of polyglottism and concludes that his mother-tongue was not Sumerian but
Akkadian. Niek Veldhuis ("How did they learn cuneiform? Tribute/Word

the points of view of structure and genre, and concludes with a new edition
of it.
This collection of essays shows very clearly the current state of the art of
reading, analysing and appreciating Sumerian literature in the academy. It is
concluded with indexes of texts cited and words discussed. The book is
handsomely produced, but in places the contents would have benefited from
more intrusive editing.

HILMI YAVUZI
Seasons of the Word: Selected Poems.
(Middle East Literature in Translation.) xiv, 118 pp. New York:
Syracuse University Press, 2007. £11.50. ISBN 0 8156 0879 9.
The publication of Seasons of the Word, a selection of Hilmi Yavuz's poems
translated by Walter G. Andrews, is an important addition to both Turkish
literature in translation and academic literature on modern Turkish poetry.
This book is an appropriate introduction to Hilmi Yavuz's erudite yet
intensely emotional poetry, admirably translated with a touch of poetic
licence which allows the translator, in his own terms, to "strike out in search
of a mood, an idiom, a pun that matches the original" (p. xiv). Walter G.
Andrews' opening remarks and Barry Tharaud's analytical afterword are
essential reading on Yavuz's poetry and help to foster the academic interests
of readers.
Andrews's insightful introduction discusses Yavuz's works in the general
context of the history of twentieth-century Turkish culture. When he refers to
the poet Behget Necatigil's view that "the excision of historical Ottoman
culture was artificial and ultimately dangerous" (p. xii), Andrews points not
only to a central theme in Hilmi Yavuz's writings but also to a still open debate
on the place of the Ottoman-Islamic heritage in secular Turkey. Hilmi Yavuz,
a poet who is as much at home in Shiraz as he is in Paris, appropriates the
Ottoman literary tradition and fuses it with a modern understanding of poetry.
Hence Yavuz's verses evoke the divans of classical Ottoman poets such as Naili
and Shaykh Galip as well as the modern divans of Federico Garcia Lorca and
Louis Aragon. Blurring temporal, cultural and geographic frontiers, Yavuz
perpetuates a poetry where the East becomes the West and the West the East,
an approach which challenges Rudyard Kipling's infamous verses and was also
advocated by other twentieth-century poets such as Ahmet Ha§im, Yahya
Kemal, Asaf Halet Ģelebi, Behget Necatigil and, to a certain extent, Nazim
Hikmet.
Laurent Mignon
