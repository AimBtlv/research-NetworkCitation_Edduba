Scribes are the providers of one of the two basic sets of data that enable us to try
and reconstruct ancient Mesopotamian history. What would we philologists be without the
fruit of their daily labours and what would the archaeologists be without the data we can
cull from the clay tablets and that complément theirs so well? The scribes were the bearers
and transmitters of such an important part of Mesopotamian culture, and yet we know so
little about them. Essays have been written about their training in which their curricula
have been reconstructed2 but basic questions remain unanswered such as who were they,
what was their position in society as trained craftsmen of a spécial kind? How specialised
were they? In whose employ were they? What did they earn? How intensive was their
labour? What were their works and how were their days? And work they did, because, as
writes Hesiod :
Both gods and men are angry with a man who lives idle, for in nature he is like the stingless
drones who waste the labour of the bees, eating without working ; but let it be your care to order your
work properly, that in the right season your barns may be full of victual. Through work men grow rich
in flocks and substance, and working they are much better loved by the immortals (303-309)3.
Our investigation into the works and days of the scribes will take us to Old
Babylonian Sippar but, although, according to Assyriological standards, this is a very well
documented period and city, our knowledge of its scribes is limited.
One of the reasons for this is the scattering of the information. We know the names
of a lot of Old Babylonian scribes but for each of them we only have few references,
moreover mostly as (last) witnesses4. There are only some twenty Old Babylonian Sippar
scribes with more than ten attestations and less than five with more than twenty. There is
only one corpus which provides a much richer harvest : the archive of the galamafes

I. THE WORKS
I. 1. Scribal work : §umum-li$i
Sumum-lisi appears for the first time in Ad 33 and witnesses a last text in As
13/8/16. His career lasted for 18 years, which is not exceptionally long for a scribe.
Notwithstanding this relatively short span, he is the best attested scribe of the Old
Babylonian period.
He is attested in 1745 texts. In 173 of these he is last witness, with the title dub.sar
or dumu.é.dub.ba.a (or none). In one text he is the debtor of leftovers from the execution
of a ritual (parsum) for Annunitum, which, incidentally, shows his social insertion in
Sippar-Amnânum. He is the son of a BëlSunu6, of whom we know nothing more, as is
shown by his seal inscription on Di 1389.
An important distinction must be made: 151 of the texts mentioning him were
found in the archive of the galamafts, 23 elsewhere. Furthermore, five texts found in the
galamab's archive do not mention them or their family. They were in fact not written for
them but for other people who had entrusted them to the galamafts7. This brings the total
of texts written for the galamafts to 146 and those written for other people('s archives) to
288, as far as the texts came down to us.
If âumum-lisi appears in 151 texts found in the archive and only 23 others this may
well be due to the fact that we have unearthed the former but of the latter we have only
isolated pieces. Prior to the discovery of the archive the statistics looked quite différent. So
much for those who claim that more texts could only be 'more of the same'.
But there is more.
The fact that Sumum-lisi wrote so many texts that were kept in the galamaft archive,
shows that they hired him to do this work. In ail of these texts the galamafos are the party
that needed the contract : creditor, lessor, or such similar function but never seller, debtor
etc. The fact that these texts were written in the galamafo's house is supported by an
archaeological argument. A large quantity of purified clay was found in the house of the
galamatjs. This was no doubt used by the scribes working in the house (TANRET 2002).
This allows us to draw the important conclusion that it was the alienating party that hired
the scribe.

But there is more.
A total of 174 is a lot compared to what we have from other scribes but what kind
of workload does this represent?
Let us consider what can be said about the density of scribal activity in the house of
the galamafrs during Sumum-lisi's professional career from Ad 33 to As 13.
I. 2. Scribal work : a matter of workload?
To obtain an indication of the scribal activity in général, as represented by the
galamab archive, we have calculated the total amount of texts written between Ad 33 and
As 13 - the career of our Sumum-lisi - and kept in this archive.
Apart from the 151 texts with Sumum-lisi's name, there are 561 texts dated from
this same period, mentioning another scribe or no scribe at ail. This adds up to 712 texts.
To this we can add 75 texts dated Ad but with broken year name. Some of these
could be earlier of course, but since 41 % of ail the Ad texts are dated from Ad 33 to 37,
whereas there are only 39 % dated in the preceding thirty-two years (and 20 % with broken
year names), the probability is in favour of the later group. Adding these 75 gives a total of
787 texts.
This is an average of a little less than 44 texts a year, or one text every eight days9.
There were lots of texts that were not dated but must have been written in this time
span. On the whole, there are some 500 undated texts in the archive. Of these, some 200
must be subtracted because they were letters, by définition written elsewhere, and sent to
the galamah's house. Turning this argument the other way round, however, there must also
have been a fair number of letters written in the galamajj's house and sent elsewhere. How
many we will never know, but we will assume, to be on the safe side, that there were as
many sent as received and add ail of the 500 to our 787 tablets.
Taking the extreme case, that ail of these undated texts were written within our 18
year time span, this would bring our total to 1287, which means 71,5 a year or one every
five days.
Finally, we know that a number of texts must have left the archive, accompanying
property sold ; that a number of them left the house, being saved during the events that
ended the occupation of the house ; that a number did not survive their more than three
millennia in the ground. How many these were, we will never know.
Even if we double the number we reached, which is without doubt, a maximum
maximorum, this means that a text was written only once every 2,5 days. Hardly a full time
occupation for a professional scribe (unless he was very well paid for each tablet he wrote)
and this would lead us to the logical conclusion that this is the reason why Sumum-lisi had
to work for other archive owners as well.
We will now take a look at his scribal infidelities : how many texts did he write for
others and what kind of texts these were. When he did this will be explained in our Part II :
the Days.

Still according to the principle that the alienating party hires a scribe to draw up the
document, we can distinguish between Sumum-lisi and the other scribes working for Ipqu

As can be seen, Sumum-lisi writes two of the silver loans (another one has no

In the case of the three other scribes we may suppose that these were not hired by
the judge, but by the other party to the transaction :
- Warad-Marduk writes a sale of real estate for the seller, Ipqu-Annunïtum is the buyer ;
- Ibbi-Sîn writes a field lease for the lessor, Ipqu-Annunïtum is the lessee ;
- Sîn-erïbam writes a document concerning the sale of a field for the seller, Ipqu
Annunïtum is the buyer.
The pattern is a little less clear, no doubt because of the limited corpus and the fact

I. 5. Scribal work : another task
As we have shown elsewhere (TANRET 2002) the archive of Ur-Utu allows not only
to assemble elements of a scribal training in reading and writing but also to put forward a
hypothesis as to the identity of the teacher and, in so doing, pinpoint another professional
activity of a scribe besides writing.
The main points are the following.
There are archaeological as well as textual remains of scribal training in the
courtyard of the house of the galama^s29. These consist of a bin, made of large bricks,
built in the floor and protruding from it. In this bin (and beside it) a group of scribal
exercises were found, together with purified clay. Comparison with other sites where
similar installations were found, confirms that the bin and the exercises are the remains of a
scribal training in the courtyard.
The stratigraphical unit to which bin and tablets belong is Illd, corresponding to the
first occupation of the house which can now be dated from Ad 29 to As 18, i.e. a period of
26 years. This period spans the last twelve years of Inanna-mansum's galamafo-ship and
nearly the whole of the career of his son, who lived in the same house. Somewhere within
this time span an apprentice was introduced into the scribe's craftsmanship. After Illd no
further training took place, the whole being sealed by a new floor.
The exercise tablets that were found point to the activity of one rather than several
apprentices and allow us to conclude that this was no général or repeated training but the
éducation of one single person.
It must be stressed that this training took place in a house that did not belong to a
master-scribe but to a galamah- If we combine this with the fact that the residual exercises
represent most of the phases of the elementary curriculum, this must mean that the person
who was educated here was someone who lived in the house and not an apprentice
following his master around, from house to house.
The broad dating of the exercises includes the last twelve years of Inanna-mansum,
before his son takes over. This allowed us to put forward the idea that, somewhere during
these years, Ur-Utu received a basic training in reading and writing skills from a
professional, as well as, of course, a training in galamafoship from his father. This gives us a
time span of twelve years within which his scribal training is to be situated.

Bëlànum as he was then called, seems somewhat anachronistic. Teaching scribal skills more
probably was done by active scribes just like this was done for other crafts. The best
candidate for this would be a scibe working often for the galamafc and we have a very
good candidate for this : our Sumum-lisi.

The use of these titles is a vexing question. As far as we can observe, many Sippar
scribes (if not ail) show the same usus : they start off as dub.sar during a good many years,
then hesitate between both titles and end by consistently using dumu.é.dub.ba.a. The
reason why they changed titles and especially why they ail hesitate a few years still éludés

promotion, but the hésitation during a few years is another matter. Why would they revert

especially intriguing since they themselves wrote their own title on the tablet. During this
transition period there must have been a very compelling reason to use the one or the
other, before the matter was definitively settled.

e. Teaching?
In the context of Sumum-lisi's activities, and following up on our hypothesis that
he could have been Ur-Utu's teacher, we can wonder if the fact that a scribe not only
fulfilled his scribal work but also started introducing young people to his craft might not
have determined the use of the title dumu.é.dub.ba.a. This title could indeed be interpreted
as a reference to the teaching institution, no longer extant in the Late Old Babylonian
Period, but referred to in a honorific title, which would have kept its pedagogical
connotation. The fact that this title was adopted after a number of years would also point in
this direction. Only experienced scribes would become 'teachers'. The alternation of titles
during the 'transition period' may also find an explanation this way. Maybe he only used
the second title when he had an apprentice-scribe with him. This could have been
occasionally during a few years and after that always. Or, having taught occasionally for a
few years, he may have been entitled to bear the second title permanently, even if he did
not constantly have an apprentice. This hypothesis leads us to another considération.
If it is true that he was Ur-Utu's teacher, this must have been somewhere before As
5, the date at which Ur-Utu became gala.maft. During this time Sumum-lisi never uses the
title dumu.é.dub.ba.a. This could be due to the fact that this was no real scribal training but
an elementary course, given to someone who would never become a professional scribe.
Only the presence of a real scribal apprentice would justify the second title. In this light we
would propose, tentatively, to translate dumu.é.dub.ba.a as 'master scribe', as opposed to
dub.sar, scribe.
The female scribes, who are now being systematically studied by B. Lion (2001 and
more to come), might add something here. They always bear the title munus dub.sar, never
munus dumu.é.dub.ba.a or dumu.munus.é.dub.ba.a. Is this because they never taught?

neither prove nor disprove our last hypotheses because their constitutive elements lie
outside of our textual documentation.

m. THE LIVELIHOOD OF THE SCRIBE
To return to the citation at the beginning of our article, scribes were not idle and no
doubt ordered their work properly. But is it true that they grew rich through work? In
other words what did they earn? Another question that cannot be answered yet and we feel
very much the truth of another line of Hesiod's text :
For the gods keep hidden firom men the means of life (The Works and the Days, 42)
The simple answer is, we don't know how (much) they were remunerated. There are - as
far as we are aware - no texts recording payments made to scribes for their work. The
reason is not hard to find : the payments must have belonged to the same category as other
household expenses which are usually considered too menial to be recorded, or were
recorded but not kept for a long time. Some tablets of this kind have corne down to us, f.i.
EDZARD 1971, 98-214 but only one of these records a payment probably to a scribe
without indication of a reason41.
If the payments were low (and irregular) this would mean that the scribes were paid
on a piece by piece basis, which is confirmed by the fact that, apart from the preferred
ones, any scribe could be called in to write a single text.
Was this enough, especially for a highly trained craftsman or woman? If not, then
they might have had another source of income, one that in earlier times probably
employed ail of them and ail or most of the other craftsmen too : a large institution, be it
temple or palace. In the later Old Babylonian Period this will no doubt have been the
palace.
If they worked in an institutional context, they must have been paid in the same
way as the other personnel, mainly in rations or maybe land grants?
But what in the case of scribes such as Sumum-lisi, whom we see mainly working
for private individuals? Would they, similarly, be integrated in (larger) private households
through a system of rations? This does not seem to be the case42. Indeed, if Inanna
mansum and after him his son Ur-Utu had fully included Sumum-lisi in their household,
binding him by regular rations, why would he then also write for other households such as
those of Ipqu-Annunïtum and Iddin-Ea, and why would they have asked other scribes to
write documents for them? What would then be the status of scribes, such as Sîn-iddinam
and Sîn-erïbam who do not write for the galamafos as often as Sumum-lisi though
nevertheless a sizeable number of times, more than they did elsewhere.
One may of course argue that even with such a well documented scribe as âumum
lisi we only have the tip of the iceberg. Indeed, the discovery of the archive of the
galamaljs has ail of a sudden provided us with 151 more attestations of him. Other, future
similar finds may augment this number significantly and maybe prove he was fully
occupied. On the other hand, simple arithmetic shows that we would need no less than
forty archives of the same size (and from the same town) for him to have written one text
every day of his 18 year long career, which would then really be a full time occupation. In
view of the frequency of scribal attestations up to now, this seems rather improbable. Ail

Comparing the incomparable
If the scribes were paid on a piece by piece basis by the private archive holders for
whom they worked and, as we deduced above, they were hired by the alienating party, it
seems logical that their fee would be incorporated in the price as a necessary part of the
alienating process. This fee would then, logically, be related to the importance of the deed
to be written, as reflected in the value of the alienated property. The calculation of this fee
lies outside of the textual documentation but we know of another 'additional payment'
which is now well documented. The SI.BI payment was explicitly written on ail late Old
Babylonian sale documents (TANRET, M. and DE GRAEF, K. 2004).
Although the nature and purpose of this SI.BI are completely différent from our
(undocumented) rémunérations of the scribes, they would share one feature : both are
payments on top of the price of the goods. In the case of the SI.BI this is a sum paid by the
buyer to the seller. In the case of the fee of the scribe, this would be paid by the one who
hired him, the alienating party, but we could well imagine that he would recuperate this by
including it in the sum owed to him by the other party. Whatever way this was done, the
point we would like to make here is that the additional SI.BI payment was considered by
both parties to be a tolerable amount. So, if we are looking for some hold on an amount
that had to be paid on top of the transaction and was deemed acceptable by both parties,
the amount of the SI.BI could give us an idea. Most of the sales of our extant corpus have
prices that yield SI.Bis of 15 and 30 Se (TANRET & DE GRAEF 2004 Appendix), being
1/12th and l/6th of a gin of silver. Going back to our calculations above where we arrived
at a text every 2,5 days or 12 a month, this would mean 1 or 2 gin of silver a month. These
numbers are of course highly conjectural and even their évaluation is problematic. We
have but a very vague idea of the cost of life in Old Babylonian Sippar and where the
boundary lay between a basic income and wealth. Furthermore these amounts have to be
considered as minima since we must not forget that scribes were indispensable participants
in the transaction process : no written contract means no valid transaction. This means that
the scribes were in a very good bargaining position and could ask much more than these
basic fees. Their demands were tempered by two factors : the transaction had to remain
economically worthwhile for the two parties concerned and a too greedy scribe could be
replaced by one of his less demanding colleagues (would this be another reason for the fact
that the archive holders occasionally hired other scribes than their usual ones?).
The général background of this would of course be some room for free enterprise,
some market like economic system.
If they did not have a basic income from fields they owned (and we know some
did, see below) an income of one or two gin of silver a month would certainly not make
rich people out of them. To give an idea, fields cost from 10 to 55 gfn, an unbuilt house
plot could be bought for 2 gin but house prices went up to 52 gin, a young cow could be
acquired for 1 1/2 gin but adult animais cost up to 15 gfn, slaves, certainly a symbol of
wealth, cost between 4 and 75 gfn (for référencés see TANRET & DE GRAEF 2004).
Our conclusion is that it is possible that scribes writing at least the number of texts
we calculated and earning nothing else than the basic fee could have lived a simple (but no
doubt intellectually fulfilling) life on earnings like this. For some, too few, we have a little
more information on their wealth.

The wealth of the scribes
We do not have much information on the wealth of the scribes. We know they
could own property since they are sometimes, not very often, mentioned as neighbours,
own a field which is leased or hire harvest labourers which also proves they owned fields.
A scribe called Awïl-Sîn cornes to our rescue (and we will come back to him further on).
We know he leased two fields, in Ad 33 (CT 8, 7a) and the next year (TCL I, 155).
Whereas the second one is a normal one eSe field, the first one is quite large : no less than 2
bur and one eSe. In fact he rents four fields in the watering district (ugârum) Pa^usum, ail
belonging to military personnel43, from the military authorities. This means he was able to
make a large investment as would no doubt be needed for such an undertaking and was of
course hoping to make a large profit. He must have been a scribe of some means.
He may well have acquired these riches through his work for wealthy Sipparians,
but there is more.
We alluded above to a possible institutional involvement of scribes. Whereas it goes
without saying that institutions must have employed a number of scribes, Utul-IStar being a
good example of one of these (as a scribe performing administrative duties, not writing the
texts), we might wonder whether it is possible that ail of our scribes were in fact
institutionally employed and only worked for private households as a supplementary
source of income. We will now undertake a limited foray into this line of scribal activity.
INSTITUTIONAL SCRIBES
In the thousands of texts of our Old Babylonian Sippar corpus scribes are of course
mentioned most often as witnesses, mostly last witnesses in the vast majority of the texts at
the end of which they record their own name. This is the case in some 600 of the Sippar
texts in our database.
In some hundred texts, however, they participate in some other way in the
transaction recorded. Among these other rôles is that of responsible officiai, defined as gir

palace wool trade or the receipt of barley delivered as state tax or biltum. Although these
references are not very numerous, they stand out as a group of thirty-four among an
otherwise very mixed and disparate bunch.

responsible scribe (ail dumu.é.dub.ba.a ) designated as gir. These are scribes functioning in
the administration.

Extended titles
In the few instances in which a spécification is added to the title dub.sar, such as
erén, di.kud, lukur or zag.ga it seems logical to suppose that this was also an indication of
their functioning in an administrative capacity, for an institution. It is remarkable that these
extensions are exclusively used in combination with the dub.sar title, never with
dumu.é.dub.ba.a. Although persons with the latter title could function in the administration
(see our remarks on gîr, above) nothing is ever added to this title. The significance of this
must be that one could be a scribe (dub.sar) of the priestesses, of the soldiers/workmen, of
the judges etc. but that the dumu.édub.ba.a title implied an added respectability which was
incompatible with any further spécification, something we tried to render through our
proposai to translate this title as master scribe which would not allow any complément
either. Our documentation is so sparse that in most cases we only have one reference each
to the scribes bearing these extended titles. For two scribes, Awïl-Adad and Marduk
muballit, we have some more information.
Awïl-Adad is attested in nine texts, from JJa 34 to Si 3048, over a period of 47
years which makes it plausible that it is indeed the same person. He uses two titles. In the
two texts dated under $ahe is dub.sar di.kud. In the Si texts he is dub.sar lukur (me§).
There is quite a gap of more than 30 years between both in which we have only one text,
dated Si 2 and where Awïl-Adad uses the simple title dub.sar.
It must be stressed that, although he is a witness in ail of these texts, he only twice is
the last witness49. In fact, in four of these texts50, there is another scribe who is làst witness
and who wrote the text. Furthermore, the witnesses of these texts are mostly, if not always,
given in the order : sanga(s) of SamaS, judges, overseer of the nadïtum priestesses, Awïl
Adad scribe of the nadïtum or of the judges... The sequence is maintained even if one or
two of the elements are missing51. In one text (CT 8, 24b) he is last witness and has no
addition to his title of dub.sar, here the sequence is : the overseer of the merchants, judges,
the overseer of the nadïtum priestesses, Awïl-Adad dub.sar. It is unclear whether this
simply means that he wrote the text or, because of the similar sequence, that he was the
dub.sar of the nadïtum priestesses but that, this being the tablet and not the case, the last
part of his title was omitted. Ail of these elements confirm that Awïl-Adad is not just a
scribe in these texts but acted as an officiai, part of an institution, as indicated by the
complément added to his title.

Concluding
At the end of our short incursion in the world of the scribes we return once more to
their works. These did not just involve the technical act of writing, especially their work in
private archives must not be underestimated. Some kinds of texts will not have been very
long to write but some others may have taken quite some time. A real estate sale did not
only require the writing of the text itself but also a search through the seller's archive for
ail relevant documentation that had to be handed over together with the sale contract. In
many cases in the later Old Babylonian period documents were missing and a certificate
had to be drawn up summarizing the history of the field and explaining which documents
were missing and why. This was at times so complex that the scribe wrote extract
documents with the relevant information from ail documents of the dossier. Some other
documents may have required more than just writing too. So if we concluded above that
the frequency of writing for an archive was not very high this must not necessarily mean
that the scribes could not be fully employed by doing this work in several archives. Their
work could even be stressful if we are to judge it by the mistake Sumum-lisi made in
writing a year formula55.
Whichever way we look, scribes were active in more than one context : in various
private households and (at least) some of them also in institutions. Both spheres of work
were by no means exclusive and a scribe could earn his living in both.
As very specialised craftsmen we may suppose (or hope?) that - apart from ail
kinds of intellectual satisfactions - they had a fair income.
In fine : Sumum-liçi
Returning finally to our Sumum-lisi, as we saw in the few texts of Iddin-Ea's
archive we have, there are slight indications for institutional work for him too. The amount
of work he did for the galamafts and other people, although it was a lot, would certainly
not have precluded this. So he too was probably active in the three spheres of scribal
éducation, writing for wealthy citizens and institutions. But even if he is the best
documented scribe of the whole Old Babylonian period it is clear that his story is certainly
not written yet. There is more, but not for now.
One thing is certain, though, through his numerous attestations and the knowledge
he has allowed us to gain, his father's wish has been granted : Sumum lisi, the son has come
out.

RÉSUMÉ
Le fait que le scribe Sumum-lisi soit attesté non moins de 151 fois dans les archives des galamafcs
d'Annunîtum à Sippar-Amnànum nous donne l'opportunité d'étudier le travail du scribe (même plusieurs fois
le même jour) dans ces archives et en-dehors où il est attesté encore 23 fois. Il a en effet travaillé pour d'autres
bourgeois de la ville et peut-être même pour l'administration. Si nous ajoutons à cela une activité
pédagogique probable (peut-être même liée au titre dont il se prévaut), nous aurions là les trois aspects du
travail d'un scribe paléo-babylonien. La question de la rémunération des scribes doit être posée mais nous
n'avons toujours que quelques éléments de réponse.
ABSTRACT
* The fact that the scribe Sumum-lisi is attested 151 times in the archive of the galama^s of Annunïtum
in Sippar-Amnânum gives us the opportunity to study the Works of a scribe (even writing several texts on the
same Day) in this archive as well as outside of it where he is attested another 23 times. He worked for other
bourgeois of the same city and maybe even for the administration. If we add to this a probable paedagogical
activity maybe even linked to the title he chooses to use.
