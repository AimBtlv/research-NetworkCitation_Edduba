Tell ed-Dër is situated about 5 kms from Abù Habbah, the site of ancient
Sippar, south-west of Baghdad. Its ancient name is not known but it was
probably one of the agglomeration of towns and settlements which clustered
round Sippar 1. The site is in the shape of a triangle with its apex to the south-
west, and it is dominated by huge earth ramparts on its western and south-
eastern sides. Since 1970 the Mission archéologique belge en Iraq has made
the site the focal point in a study of the area.
In his Avant- Propos (pp. vii-ix), L. De Meyer, the director of the project,
draws attention to the fact that whereas Tell ed-Dēr I (1971) was intended as
the first of a series of yearly preliminary reports, the present volume is to be
considered as a Progress report (as opposed to a Final report) dealing with
some of the results of the first seven seasons of work at Tell ed-Dēr. Some
of the subjects which will be dealt with in subsequent volumes are listed on
p. viii and include the results of soundings at Abu Habbah, now published
as Tell ed-Dèr III (Leuven 1980).
The volume has been expensively produced, a fact which is unfortunately
reflected in its price. The page size is convenient and the lay-out is well spaced
with indented sections leaving references isolated in wide margins. There
are clearly defined "chapters" divided into sections and sub-sections numbered
in sequence for easy reference. Each contribution is written as a separate
article with its own bibliography (even listing other contributions in the same
volume), abbreviations, and figures numbered independently. These articles
are written in French, German or English, but the checking of the English
manuscript by a native English speaker with a knowledge of geology and ar-
chaeology might have prevented the occurrence of some rather awkward turns
of phrase and an addiction to technical jargon which make the text rather
heavy going in parts. Such sentences as:
"If the presence of an important site such as Tell Abù Habbah proofs
of the possibility of settlement in the crevasse area, the accumulation
of mollusc clay all around the site is witnessing, instead, of its position
as an artificial island in the time following the A-system deposition"
(p. 51)

The first article or chapter deals with the ramparts which surround Tell
ed-Dēr, and with the sequence of floods which made them necessary (pp. 1-35),
written by R. Paepe, H. Gasche and L. De Meyer. In an interesting discussion
of the climatic background to the site (pp. 1-9 and Table 1) we are told that
a late Ubaid flood deposit at Ur was followed by flood deposits at Ur, Kish
and Shuruppak (c. 2900-2350 B.c. - Sidney Smith's chronology is used, see
p. 21) which are related to "floods recorded in the same timespan in both the
Indus and Nile valleys" and which "may perhaps reflect a palaeoclimatical
origin of world-wide impact" (pp. 4-6). At Tell ed-Dēr there is evidence for
a rise in water level of 6 m in less than two centuries between 1830 and 1650
b.c. (the "middle and late palaeo- Babylonian Epochs") and it is suggested
that a "world-wide climatical event seems most likely at the origin of these
floods, causing a rise in sea-level as well" (p. 24). This led to the building and
subsequent heightening of the artificial dams which protected the site from
flooding and through the western part of which sounding B was cut. Archaeo-
logical evidence for the dating of the different phases of the "wall" is given
on pp. 18-20, but in this connection it might have been useful to add the evid-
ence of the texts 2. A further series of floods, recorded in Chad and the Indus
valley during the second half of the 2nd millennium b.c., also have their Meso-
potamian counterparts at Tell ed-Dēr (pp. 6-7, 30). It is suggested that
the "unfinished third side of the wall is perhaps connected with the abandon-
ment of the major occupation of the city" probably at the very end of the
2nd millennium b.c. since "the latest large settlement of Tell ed-Dēr which
demanded an undertaking of such importance was certainly during the meso-
Babylonian Period" (p. 30). There seems to have been a considerable drop
in the water level after this but from the late 6th century b.c. onwards there
is once again evidence of flooding which can be tied in with Greek floods and
changes in sea level. The end of this period of flooding is dated c. 50 b.c. on
p. 8 and c. A.D. 200 for Greece and A.D. 300 for Tell ed-Dēr on p. 31 (cf. Table 1
and pp. 32-33). It is suggested (p. 9) that a period of drought in the 7th cen-
tury A.D. might have triggered off the Arab migrations and the beginning of
the Islamic era.

By far the longest section is that by H. Gasche on a group of buildings
known as "l'Ensemble I" in sounding A (pp. 57-131) on the western part of
the mound. The upper levels of these buildings had already been discussed
in Tell ed-Dèr I, and on p. 58 we find the correlation between the strata and
levels used then and those now in use (Ia-e) . On p. 58 ff . we also find definitions
of the terms used, some of them adopted and adapted from geological voca-
bulary (e.g. "lithologique", "litho-organique", "géostratigraphie" as opposed
to "archéostratigraphie", "surface isochrone"). The "Ensemble I" was re-
built several times and the different phases are dealt with twice over - once
from a "chronostratigraphical" and "lithostratigraphical" point of view
(pp. 61-69), and once for the architectural remains (p. 69 ff.); these two ap-
proaches could perhaps have been combined to some profit.
The central building of "Ensemble I" probably formed one property
and it was built over the partially excavated remains of earlier levels which
had been subsequently filled in to varying depths (Phases k 1 and 2). Other
examples of this practice are discussed on pp. 72-73. In this context it should
be pointed out that there seem to have been two different traditions present
in Mesopotamia from early times and both are exemplified at Uruk: the White
Temple was built on a high platform which enclosed the ruins of previous shrines
built on the same site, while the buildings of the nearby Eanna precinct were
almost completely levelled before new construction was undertaken following
a totally different lay-out. A foundation deposit consisting of a bird, prob-
ably a goose, lying on baked bricks and covered with potsherds, was laid below
Ik 2, i.e. before the construction of the central building started (p. 74).
Textual references to geese, which are often connected with the goddess Bau,
have been collected together by H. Piesl-Trenkwalder on pp. 133-135.
Pottery and the discovery of three dated tablets in Phase Ig have enabled
the sequence of buildings in the Ensemble I to be attributed to the Old Baby-
lonian period, probably extending from the first half of the 19th century b.c.
to about 1700 B.c. (pp. 126-128). The central building was frequently rebuilt
but its plan changed little; the area surrounding it, however, was subject to
numerous alterations - some parts being now roofed, now turned into court-
yards, now subdivided by walls, now opened out, and sometimes destroyed
altogether by pits which may have been dug to provide material for the
manufacture of mud-brick (the excavators draw attention to a present-day
building sequence to substantiate this suggestion - pp. 100-101). Finally
in Phase Ic, virtually only the central building remained.
This central building was a house built round a courtyard, with access
to an upper storey but with no evidence for a balcony round the courtyard as
at Ur. There was a complicated arrangement of drains, particularly in the
early phases, and pots were buried in doorways (rather inconveniently one
would have thought) to collect rain-water (see Plan 7, profile C under 2 for
the most elaborate of these in section and cf. Pl. 25). Walls were rebuilt
following the same alignment, in most cases with brick made of reworked
archaeological débris. The emplacement of doorways was subject to variation

In later phases the number of burials in the central building (but not in the
rest of the sounding) dropped markedly and the excavators concluded that
its inhabitants must have been buried elsewhere. Altogether 148 burials were
excavated in sounding A and since two- thirds of these belonged to children and
babies, it seems that infant mortality was high (there seems to be no question
of child sacrifice - p. 81). Burials were generally of types we would expect
for Mesopotamia though vaulted tombs were not found, but a type of shaft-
grave is also recorded and foreign parallels are cited for this (see Plan 7, pro-
file C under 2 - T. 42), and under 22 - T. 100, for sections). The burials
are discussed in connection with each phase and also on pp. 87-90. The later,
post-occupational burials are catalogued on pp. 108-117 (and Fig. 13) and are
attributed to a late Parthian or early Sassanian cemetery (pp. 117-118).
A discussion and a selective catalogue of the pottery and objects found
in the Ensemble I is presented on pp. 119-126 with cross-references to Tell
ed-Dēr I. The excavations have produced a closely dated sequence of pot-
tery types from which it transpires that painted decoration is restricted to the
upper phases (Ie onwards). On p. 103 we read that a large quantity of pottery
was strewn over loci 16 and 17 north of the central house in Phase Ic, but un-
fortunately no details are given and only one pot from this area and phase
is illustrated (PI. 27-7). A row of inverted pots below Phase Ie seems to have
served for drainage (p. 101 and PL 11-1). In Phase la, in the north-east corner
of the sounding, a series of rooms was excavated, one of which contained another
group of inverted pots but no explanation could be found for this. Among
a fairly typical collection of terracottas was one of an emaciated seated male
figure (Pl. 27-1). Edith Porada published a bronze depicting an identical
figure in the round4, which she identified with the demon of death Kubu on
the basis of the plaques referred to on p. 124. A date for the bronze figure
in the reign of Apil-Sin was suggested - a date which would accord fairly well
with the find-place of the Tell ed-Dēr terracotta. A small duckshaped hae-
matite weight and an inscribed bronze adze complete the inventory of objects
catalogued in this section (pp. 122-126 and Pis. 26-28).

reared for milk and as draught animals. In this connection, mention could
be made of the representation of cattle used for ploughing on Akkadian seals 19 .
Since the faunai remains consist almost exclusively of domesticated species,
hunting and fishing (two fish samples) obviously played virtually no part at
all in the economy of the site.
A catalogue by A. Gautier of the molluscs found from 1970-1975 com-
pletes the studies of material from the excavations (pp. 191-194). Some were
probably fossils from the alluvial deposits on the site but all the others came
from the Indian Ocean, probably by trade, and were mostly used for
jewellery.

The last two sections of the book deal with photographic methods of
recording used on the site. L. Hardegen of the Swiss firm Wild, and H. Gasche
discuss "an experimental approach to the possible application of terrestial
photogrammetry in surveying archaeological ruins" (pp. 195-209 and Pis. 33-
37), but do not mention that the same firm had already sold a camera in 1971
to another Belgian team working at Apamea on the Orontes, in Syria 20 . The
three-dimensional recording of that huge, stone-built, ruined city justified the
financial outlay which the acquisition of a photogramme trie camera involved.
The plotting of the stereoscopic pairs was extremely expensive so that only
those directly relevant to a season's work or restoration could be sent away
for processing and the resulting pencil drawing then had to be inked in with
the aid of "normal" close-up photographs21. Whether the expense is justified
on a site where mud-brick is the building material, is another matter. In
addition, the necessity for a photographic tower (not needed at Apamea) res-
tricted the use of the photogrammetric survey and the tower had to be moved
eight times by 10 to 15 workmen, while the cameras and control points had
to be reset each time (pp. 205 and 207) in order to produce the plan of one
level in sounding E. Also, for the whole of this time, and until the plates had
been developed, archaeological work had to remain at a standstill. Not only
do the stereoscopic pairs still have to be plotted in a specially equipped labora-
tory in Europe (PI. 35-2) but the pencil plans obtained as a result have
to be "completed on the basis of data surveyed from the original" according
to traditional methods (p. 204). The section and plan (pp. 206 and 207, Figs. 6
and 7) might indeed have taken a number of days to produce by these same
traditional methods, but against this we must set the price of the camera, film,
processing and plotting, the cost of employing 10 to 15 workmen for two days
to move the tower, and the time needed in recording details by conventional
methods which are less affected by adverse weather conditions than a photo-
grammetric survey. The suggestion, on p. 209, of "collective equipment"
and a "pool of instruments" only makes sense if expeditions can excavate at
different times of the year within the same political frontiers, since transporting
such equipment from one country to another, with all the paper-work and
customs formalities involved, would waste far more man-hours than it might
