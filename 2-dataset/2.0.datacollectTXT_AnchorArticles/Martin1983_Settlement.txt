SETTLEMENT PATTERNS AT SHURUPPAK
By HARRIET P. MARTIN
In the third millennium b.c. Shuruppak (modern Fara) was a major city located
on the Euphrates in central Sumer. Today the mound of the ancient city extends
for over a kilometre north to south. Including the high mound and the lower rise
of one to two metres surrounding it, Shuruppak covers about 120 hectares. Only
about 35 hectares rise about the 3 m contour, however, and nowhere does the
mound rise much above 9 m.
There have been two major excavations at Shuruppak, by the D.O.G. in 1902?
they crisscrossed the mound with trenches but recorded lamentably little strati-
graphy?and by the University of Pennsylvania in 1931?when Erich Schmidt
conducted limited excavations at four points on the mound. It is clear from these
excavations that Shuruppak was inhabited from about 3000 to 2000 b.c.1
In 1973 I visited the site and conducted a three-day surface survey with the help
of my husband Christopher Martin, the British School of Archaeology in Iraq (then
under the direction of Diana Kirkbride), and the Iraqi Directorate of Antiquities,
with the aim of enlarging our knowledge of the settlement patterns at Shuruppak.
The results of that survey shed new light on the size of the city of Shuruppak in
the different phases of the third millennium. Excavations to date have concentrated
on the higher levels of the mound. The major question which we hoped to answer
concerned the origin of the debris below the 3 m contour. Was it, for example,
building remains from short periods when the city expanded beyond the main
mound or did it represent remains from outer suburbs and gardens in use at all
times but with a low population density ? 2
Because of the size of this huge mound, a systematic sampling of sherds over the
entire mound was impossible in the three days available to us. We therefore con-
centrated on those areas least known from the earlier excavations, primarily the
lower slopes of the mound below the 3 m contour. In this area a ranging pole was
set up at random points and all diagonostic sherds within 2 m of the pole were
collected. The sherd density is so thick on the surface at Shuruppak that the typical
collecting point yielded over sixty diagnostic sherds. Sherds were collected at
twenty such points around the mound base. In addition four collections were made
in the area of the German trenches to confirm that the pottery here was comparable
with the published finds from these areas.
We shall review the evidence provided by the survey, period by period.
Jamdat Nasr and Early Dynastic I Periods (Fig. 1, Map 1)

Many Jamdat Nasr and ED I pottery types are very similar. Therefore in the
survey the two periods were treated together. Material of this date occurred at all
collection points on the main mound and on the northern and eastern slopes.
Shuruppak would appear therefore to have already been a large city covering as
much as 70 hectares by at least the end of ED I.4 The survey conclusions are backed
up by material from the earlier excavations. In these Jamdat Nasr polychrome
painted sherds and pots came from squares DF, FG, GH, HF, and HH.5
Similarly, findpoints of solid footed goblets confirm the large size of the city in
ED I. The 1973 survey found sherds from solid footed goblets at three points on
the low north slopes of the mound and at all four collection points on the main
mound itself. Apart from these collecting points, solid footed goblets were also
noted at two points in the central depression of the main mound and twice on the
higher mound to the south (near the middle of the D.O.G. trench XI and
trench XII).
In general in this survey there was nearly always some trace of JN-ED I material
wherever later ED II?III sherds were found. The only exceptions were four
samples collected west of the main mound and on the western side of the main
mound. The two more northerly samples had no JN-ED I sherds ; the two further
south had only one ED I sherd each. It therefore seems likely that the JN-ED I
settlement started under the main mound and spread to the north and east.
Early Dynastic II-III (Fig. 1, Map 2)
Although it may be possible to distinguish ED II from ED Ilia when a large
collection of stratified material is available, the most common pottery types are
very similar in each period and it is therefore impractical to try to distinguish
ED II and Ilia surface sherds.6 Sherds most often found in ED II/IIIa settlement
areas are the string-cut bases of the conical bowls and cups, the straight, plain
spouts from the spouted jars, and the slightly flaring rimless necks from the jars
and cups.

Despite this erosion, there is some evidence that the ED III city extended further
to the west than the earlier settlements. As noted above, four collecting points on
the western slopes of the mound yielded ED II?III pottery with either nothing
earlier or, in two instances, with only one JN-ED I sherd. The ED III settlement
also extended down the eastern slope of the mound. Shuruppak does therefore
appear to have expanded from the JN/ED I phase, when it may have had a
maximum size of 70 hectares, to the ED II and III periods when it seems to have
reached a maximum size of about 100 hectares.
It cannot be proved that the city of Shuruppak had a city wall at this period.
The D.O.G. found traces of what appears to be a city wall in the small mound south-
west of the main mound at NC and OC.9 The 1973 surface survey found only
Ur III pottery in the area of this mound. Similarly the other small mounds ringing
the main mound proved consistently to be either Ur III or very late (eighteenth
century a.d.) in date.10
Akkadian and Ur III Periods (Fig. 1, Map 3)
Shuruppak was certainly inhabited continuously until the end of the third
millennium b.c. Akkadian seal impressions were found by Schmidt in FG 42/43.
He also found Akkadian pottery and a tablet in a silo (his Pit II) located near his
excavations in HI 47-48.n This is insufficient to indicate the size of the Akkadian
city but its existence is beyond doubt.
The evidence for an extensive Ur III settlement is in fact not surprising as we
know from the contemporary texts that Shuruppak was an important city in the
Ur III period. Numerous texts refer to ensi's of Shuruppak under the Ur III kings ;
we know the names of nine of these men.12 By far the most interesting Ur III text
referring to Shuruppak is the Drehern harvest list which states that 1,200 men were
recruited from Shuruppak compared to 1,800 from Adab and 2,600 from Umma for
harvesting work.13 Professor Adams estimates the size of the latter cities at about

In practice, however, it is difficult to estimate the size of the city in the Akkadian
and Ur III periods. Very heavy wind erosion has meant that most levels later than
ED Ilia have been worn away on the main mound where both of the major
excavations have concentrated. In both the D.O.G. and University of Pennsylvania
excavations, finds later than ED Ilia are known only from fill in pits and silos. The
lower slopes of the mound to the north, west and south produced an abundance of
Akkadian and Ur III pottery, however. I have not attempted to distinguish
between pots of these two periods since there is little well published material from
this period and, from what there is, it seems clear that Ur III pottery was often
similar to Akkadian pottery. Fig. 2 illustrates typical sherds classified as Akkadian/
Ur III.
At seven points spread along the western and southern edges of the mound
Akkadian and Ur III sherds were found with ED material. In these collections
the later pottery makes up at least 7% to 25% of the sherds sampled. In addition
one sherd of a collar-rimmed bowl (Fig. 2, no. 1) came from the collection point
at the northern tip of the mound. Nothing but Ur III pottery was found on the
hillocks south-west of the mound, including the " city wall " mound. The indications
are, therefore, that the Ur III settlement was concentrated along the western half
of the mound. The absence of Ur III sherds on the higher slopes of the mound
may be due to wind and salt actually crumbling these sherds to dust in this very
exposed area.15 Many sherds from the collar-rimmed bowls typical of the Akkadian
to Ur III Periods were noticed next to a silo just north of the D.O.G. trench VII.
This corresponds with Schmidt's observation that the fill of the silos is usually
Akkadian to Ur III in date. (It is of course possible that the Ur III settlement was
limited to the western slope of the mound but used the silos elsewhere on the mound
for rubbish tips.)
If only the western half of the mound was occupied in Ur III times, it seems
probable that the city covered an area of about 50 hectares at this time. If habitation
continued on the higher central mound as well, but has since eroded, the Ur III
city could have covered a total of 85 hectares in all. If this were the case, Shuruppak
would have shrunk only slightly from its ED III maximum of 100 hectares. As the
only sherds found on the hillock where the D.O.G. recovered remains of the large
" city wall " were Ur III in date, quite possibly the wall was not built until this
period (see fn. 9).

out on two sides in order to make room for large public buildings and further
houses, however. The Ur III temple and palace and the later audience hall of
Naramsin were constructed at a height of only 4 m above plain level.16 These
structures, being at a considerably lower level than the main mound, suffered much
less from the wind and rain which denuded the higher mound.
It seems probable that Shuruppak grew according to a similar pattern at this
time. In the case of Shuruppak, however, no large scale excavations have been
carried out away from the highly eroded main mound and, in consequence, the
impression has remained of a city which virtually died after ED III. Quite probably,
therefore, the picture which the excavations have given us (that the city was very
small in the Akkadian and Ur III periods) is totally misleading for both the Ur III
and the Akkadian periods.
Isin-Larsa Period
Remains postdating the Ur III period are so few that, lacking written evidence
to the contrary, we must presume that the Isin-Larsa city of Shuruppak consisted
of a handful of houses. Schmidt found one cylinder seal of Isin-Larsa date near the
surface in HI 48.17 Of the D.O.G. finds, only a small, lugged pot can be dated to
this period.18 Abu Hatab (ancient Kisurra) to the north replaced Shuruppak as the
main local centre at this time. The shifting course of the Euphrates may well have
been responsible for the final abandonment of Shuruppak.
Islamic Settlement (Fig. 1, Map 4)
It remains only to add a few words about relatively recent occupations of the site.
Small mounds to both the east and west of the main mound were covered with
very late (eighteenth century a.d.) pottery of a distinctive gritty type. Several
collections failed to produce earlier sherds on these mounds and it therefore seems
likely that these hillocks are entirely later settlement and have no connection with
the original city of Shuruppak (see fn. 10). On the plan these mounds appear to
continue the line of the city wall found in the Ur III mound south-west of the
main mound. Excavation might indeed produce wall remains at the centre of these
tells, but it must be admitted that all the settlement debris visible is of a much
later period.
Conclusions
To summarize, Shuruppak was founded no later than the Jamdat Nasr period.
It soon grew to about 70 hectares in size and maintained this size in the ED I
period. The settlement spread under the main mound and extended to the north
and east. In ED II?III Shuruppak covered the same area but also expanded to
the west to make a total area of about 100 hectares. Akkadian-Ur III Shuruppak
