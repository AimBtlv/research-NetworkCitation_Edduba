## Overview 
A central contribution of this study lies not in the reconstruction of Edubba history, but in the visual operationalisation of epistemic relations within scholarly discourse. By transforming citation relationships into a multi-layered visual system, the analysis makes visible structural properties that remain inaccessible through traditional close reading or bibliographic review.
Across all five figures, a consistent design system was employed, enabling cross-figure visual comparison of epistemic roles. This consistency is methodologically significant: it allows the viewer to interpret Step 1–Step 5 not as isolated analytical categories, but as a continuous spatial and visual encoding of epistemic distance from primary data.

---
#### H1 From hierarchy to fragmentation
---
H1
---
(H1–Figure 1)  
Figure 1 demonstrates that the citation network does not approximate a hierarchical structure. The dominance of Step 5 nodes, clearly visible in both bar and donut representations, immediately contradicts the expected tree-like model.
Importantly, this conclusion does not depend on numerical output alone. The redundancy of visual encodings (bar length + proportional area) ensures that the absence of hierarchy is perceptually robust: even without reading values, the structure is immediately identifiable as non-hierarchical.
This highlights a broader methodological point: hierarchical epistemic models may persist in theory, but fail under visual structural inspection of citation systems.

#### H2 Primary sources as epistemic dead ends

---
 (H2–Figure 2)

Figure 2 reveals one of the most important structural findings of the study: primary archaeological sources (Step 1) are not integrated into the flow of scholarly mediation.
The node-link visualization is crucial here. Unlike tabular metrics, the spatial separation between Step 1 and Step 3 clusters makes the structural disconnection immediately perceptible. The absence of connecting edges is itself a visual result.
A key insight emerges from combining Panels A–C:
Step 1 sources are cited (non-zero in-degree)
but have zero betweenness
This duality is visually reinforced: they are present in the network but absent from its connectivity structure. In epistemic terms, they function as endpoints rather than transmission channels.

#### Mediators as structural bottlenecks 

---
(H3–Figure 3)
Figure 3 demonstrates that epistemic flow is highly concentrated within a small subset of Step 2 nodes. The Lorenz-like accumulation curve in Panel B is particularly important: it visually encodes extreme inequality in betweenness distribution.
This is a key methodological strength of the visualization: it transforms abstract centrality concentration into an immediately interpretable geometric pattern.
The result is not merely quantitative but structural:
epistemic connectivity is not distributed
it is compressed into a minimal bridging layer
The visual design (uniform color for Step 2 nodes) further reinforces this interpretation by removing categorical noise and emphasising internal rank only.

#### Displacement of influence
---
(H4–Figure 4)
Figure 4 reveals a counterintuitive pattern: eigenvector centrality is not aligned with proximity to primary data.
The heatmap representation is crucial here, because it preserves both:
network topology (who connects to whom)
scalar intensity (eigenvector magnitude)
This dual encoding makes visible a structural mismatch:
Step 1 nodes occupy peripheral, low-intensity regions
Step 3 nodes dominate high-density eigenvector clusters
The anomaly (Kramer 1949) is particularly important because it is visually isolated: it appears as a high-intensity node in a structurally low-centrality class. This makes it an epistemic outlier rather than a category contradiction.
Thus, visual analysis supports the conclusion that epistemic influence is decoupled from empirical proximity.


#### Reliability as a rare structural configuration 
---
(H5–Figure 5)
Figure 5 provides the most complex visual argument of the study. The Trust Triangle is inherently a topological object, and therefore requires a network-based visualization rather than a scalar representation.
The key methodological point is that reliability is defined not by individual attributes, but by closed relational configurations. This makes the visualization indispensable: the concept cannot be understood without seeing triangular closure.
Two findings emerge visually:
Reliable sources are extremely rare (visually sparse red nodes)
They cluster in Step 2 rather than Step 3
This is particularly significant because it directly contradicts the intuitive expectation that authority (Step 3) corresponds to reliability. Instead, the visualization shows that reliability emerges from structural mediation patterns, not citation popularity.
